Volume II · Part I · The Physical Cloud — Chapter 4

Datacenter Networks for AI

An AI cluster is thousands of accelerators that must behave, for some purposes, as one machine. What makes that possible is the network: a hierarchy of switches and cables, carrying RDMA traffic at hundreds of gigabits per second per GPU, arranged in topologies whose properties can be derived exactly. This chapter explains the two networks of an AI datacenter, the mathematics of fat trees and rail-optimised designs, the transports that move bytes between GPUs without involving their CPUs, why flows collide and how that halves throughput, the boundary between scale-up and scale-out, and what inference—as opposed to training—asks of the network.

Why this chapter

Volume I treated the network as a pair of numbers. In Vol. I Chapter 17 we modelled a link by a latency \(\alpha\) and a bandwidth \(\beta_{\text{link}}\), derived the cost of ring and tree all-reduces, and observed that NVLink inside a server is about nine times faster than the network between servers. In Vol. I Chapter 13 we needed the per-GPU network bandwidth \(\beta_{\text{net}}\) to decide whether moving a KV cache between prefill and decode machines was cheap enough. Those models assumed that the network delivers its rated bandwidth to every pair of GPUs that wants it. Real networks do not. Whether they come close depends on how the switches are wired, how traffic is spread over the wires, and how the transport reacts when two flows want the same wire.

The network is also one of the most expensive parts of an AI cluster—optics, switches and cabling commonly account for 10–20% of its capital cost—and one of the hardest to operate, because its failures are partial and its performance problems look like slow GPUs. Infrastructure interviews ask about it directly ("how many switches does a 16,000-GPU cluster need?", "what is a rail-optimised topology?", "why do we use RDMA?", "RoCE or InfiniBand?") and indirectly, whenever a design moves data between machines.

We begin by separating the networks an AI datacenter contains (§4.1). We then derive how large non-blocking networks are built from small switches—Clos networks and fat trees—and what they cost (§4.2), and how the rail-optimised designs used in AI clusters exploit the structure of GPU traffic (§4.3). We explain the transports, RDMA over InfiniBand, RoCE and cloud-specific fabrics (§4.4), and then the central performance problem of these networks, congestion and the collisions of hashed flows, which we analyse quantitatively (§4.5). We contrast scale-up and scale-out domains (§4.6), catalogue what inference needs from the network (§4.7), and close with the front-end network and its costs (§4.8).

Learning objectives
  1. Distinguish the front-end, back-end, scale-up and management networks of an AI datacenter and the traffic each carries.
  2. Derive the number of hosts and switches in two- and three-tier Clos (fat-tree) networks from the switch radix, and define bisection bandwidth and oversubscription.
  3. Explain rail-optimised and rail-only topologies and why they suit GPU traffic.
  4. Explain RDMA, and compare InfiniBand, RoCEv2 and cloud transports (EFA/SRD) in reliability, congestion control and operational burden.
  5. Quantify the throughput lost to ECMP hash collisions for synchronous collectives and list the remedies.
  6. Decide which parallelism and data movement belong on scale-up versus scale-out links, and estimate the network load of disaggregation, expert parallelism and weight loading.

Uses: collectives and the α–β model (Vol. I Chapter 17), parallelism (Vol. I Chapter 18), disaggregation and KV transfer (Vol. I Chapter 13), MoE expert parallelism (Vol. I Chapter 19), and Chapters 1–3 of this volume.

4.1 Two networks, and two more

A conventional datacenter has one network: Ethernet links from every server to a top-of-rack switch, and a hierarchy of switches above, carrying TCP/IP traffic between servers and to the internet. An AI datacenter keeps that network and adds another, and the reason is a difference in what the traffic needs. Ordinary traffic—API requests, database queries, storage reads—consists of many small, independent flows that tolerate occasional delays and losses, and a server's share of the network is perhaps 25–100 Gb/s. GPU-to-GPU traffic consists of a few enormous, synchronised transfers: a collective in which every GPU sends gigabytes at once, all of which must finish before any GPU can continue. It needs an order of magnitude more bandwidth per server, very low and predictable latency, and no packet loss, since a lost packet stalls a whole collective.

The result is the arrangement of Figure 4.1. The front-end network is the ordinary datacenter Ethernet: it connects each server through one or two NICs at 100–400 Gb/s to the rest of the provider's network, and carries user requests and responses, storage traffic (model weights from object storage, logs), control-plane traffic and anything to and from the internet. The back-end network, also called the compute fabric or the GPU fabric, connects each GPU through its own NIC at 400–800 Gb/s to a separate, isolated set of switches, and carries only accelerator traffic: collectives, pipeline activations, KV-cache transfers, expert-parallel dispatch. Inside each server or rack, the scale-up network—NVLink and NVSwitch, or a TPU's inter-chip interconnect—joins accelerators at several times the back-end bandwidth. And a slow out-of-band management network reaches every server's baseboard management controller, so that machines can be powered, reimaged and diagnosed even when everything else is broken.

The separation of front-end and back-end is not merely tidy. It isolates the performance-critical, loss-intolerant GPU traffic from the bursty, loss-tolerant general traffic; it lets each network use the transport and congestion control that suit its traffic; and it limits the blast radius of failures and attacks, since the back-end is reachable only from GPU NICs. The cost is two sets of switches and cables, and the back-end set is much the larger: with one 400 Gb/s NIC per GPU, a 16,000-GPU cluster has 6.4 Pb/s of back-end injection bandwidth, more than the front-end networks of many entire datacenters. How such a network is built from switches of a few dozen ports each is a beautiful piece of combinatorics.

4.2 Building a large switch from small ones: Clos networks and fat trees

The ideal network for a cluster would be a single switch with one port per GPU, through which any GPU could send to any other at full rate, whatever the other GPUs were doing. No such switch exists for thousands of ports: a single switch chip of 2024–2025 has a total capacity of about 51.2 Tb/s, which is 64 ports at 800 Gb/s or 128 at 400 Gb/s. The number of ports on a switch is its radix, \(k_{\text{sw}}\). The question is how to wire many radix-\(k_{\text{sw}}\) switches together so that the whole behaves like one large switch.

The answer dates from 1953, when Charles Clos, an engineer at Bell Labs, showed how to build large telephone exchanges from small crossbar switches arranged in stages. A Clos network has an input stage, a middle stage and an output stage of small switches, with every input-stage switch connected to every middle-stage switch, and every middle-stage switch to every output-stage switch. Clos proved that if each input switch has \(n\) inputs and there are at least \(2n-1\) middle switches, any idle input can always be connected to any idle output without disturbing existing connections; Beneš later showed that \(n\) middle switches suffice if existing connections may be rearranged. Modern packet-switched datacenter networks are "folded" Clos networks, in which the input and output stages are the same physical switches, and they are usually called leaf–spine or fat-tree networks.

Figure 4.2 shows the simplest case, a two-tier leaf–spine network. Each leaf switch (also called top-of-rack or tier-1) uses half of its ports for hosts and half for links up to spines. Each spine switch connects to every leaf. If every leaf has \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) uplinks, one to each of \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) spines, and every spine has \(k_{\text{sw}}\) ports, one to each of \(k_{\text{sw}}\) leaves, the network has \(k_{\text{sw}}\) leaves, \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) spines and \(k_{\text{sw}}\cdot k_{\text{sw}}/2\) host ports.

Figure 4.2's key property is that every pair of leaves is joined through every spine, by \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) distinct paths of two hops each. If the leaves have as much uplink capacity as host capacity, the network can carry any pattern in which each host sends and receives at most its link rate—provided the traffic is spread evenly over the paths. To grow beyond \(k_{\text{sw}}^2/2\) hosts, we add a tier. In the three-tier fat tree of Al-Fares, Loukissas and Vahdat (2008), switches are grouped into pods: each pod is a two-tier network of \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) leaves and \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) aggregation switches, and the pods are joined by a layer of core switches[1].

Proposition 4.1 — Size of non-blocking Clos networks

Built from switches of radix \(k_{\text{sw}}\) with half of each lower-tier switch's ports facing down, a non-blocking two-tier leaf–spine network supports \(k_{\text{sw}}^2/2\) hosts using \(3k_{\text{sw}}/2\) switches, and a three-tier fat tree supports \(k_{\text{sw}}^3/4\) hosts using \(5k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\) switches.

Proof. Two tiers: each spine has \(k_{\text{sw}}\) ports, one per leaf, so there are \(k_{\text{sw}}\) leaves; each leaf has \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) uplinks, one per spine, so there are \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) spines; hosts are \(k_{\text{sw}}\cdot k_{\text{sw}}/2\); switches \(k_{\text{sw}}+k_{\text{sw}}/2\). Three tiers: a pod has \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) leaves, each with \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) hosts, so \(k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\) hosts and \(k_{\text{sw}}\) switches. Each aggregation switch has \(k_{\text{sw}}/2\) uplinks, so a pod has \(k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\) uplinks; each core switch has one link to each pod, so there are \(k_{\text{sw}}\) pods and \(k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\) core switches. Hosts: \(k_{\text{sw}}\cdot k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\); switches: \(k_{\text{sw}}\cdot k_{\text{sw}}+k_{\text{sw}}^2/4=5k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\). \(\square\)

Proposition 4.1 turns network design into arithmetic. With 64-port switches at 400 Gb/s (InfiniBand NDR generation), two tiers reach 2,048 GPUs and three tiers 65,536. With 128-port switches, two tiers reach 8,192 and three tiers more than half a million. Figure 4.3 plots these limits. Because each tier adds latency, cost and failure points, designers use the fewest tiers that reach the cluster size, which is one reason switch radix is the most important property of a switch chip.

Two further quantities describe a network's capability. Its bisection bandwidth is the minimum, over all ways of cutting the hosts into two equal halves, of the total bandwidth of the links crossing the cut. A non-blocking fat tree of \(H\) hosts with link rate \(r\) has bisection bandwidth \(Hr/2\): half the hosts can send to the other half at full rate. Its oversubscription ratio is the ratio of downlink to uplink capacity at a tier: a leaf with 48 host ports and 16 uplinks of the same speed is 3:1 oversubscribed, so hosts under it can together send only a third of their capacity off the leaf. Front-end networks are routinely oversubscribed 3:1 or more, because general traffic rarely uses full rate everywhere at once. AI back-end networks are usually built non-blocking (1:1), because collectives do use full rate everywhere at once—though, as the next section shows, the structure of GPU traffic allows some savings.

Cost scales with the number of links, and every link above the leaf is an optical cable with a transceiver at each end. A non-blocking three-tier fat tree has, per host, one host link plus two links per host at each higher tier boundary, so roughly five transceivers per GPU; at several hundred dollars to over a thousand dollars per 800 Gb/s transceiver, optics alone can cost thousands of dollars per GPU, and the optics also draw power (about 15 W per 800 Gb/s module) and fail more often than any other network component. Reducing tiers and links is therefore worth real money, and GPU traffic patterns make that possible.

Drill 4.1 — Switches for a 16,384-GPU cluster

Design a non-blocking back-end for 16,384 GPUs with 64-port switches. How many tiers, pods and switches are needed, and how many host-facing and switch-to-switch links?

Solution. Two tiers support only 2,048 hosts, so three tiers are needed; a full three-tier fat tree with \(k_{\text{sw}}=64\) supports 65,536, so we build a partial one with \(16{,}384/(64^2/4)=16\) pods. Each pod has 32 leaves and 32 aggregation switches (1,024 hosts); 16 pods give 1,024 switches. Each aggregation switch has 32 uplinks, so 16 pods have \(16\times32\times32=16{,}384\) uplinks; each core switch takes one link from each of the 16 pods per port group—with 64 ports, each core switch can connect to 16 pods with 4 links each, so \(16{,}384/64=256\) core switches. Total about 1,280 switches. Links: 16,384 host links, 16,384 leaf–aggregation links and 16,384 aggregation–core links, about 49,000 links in all, three per GPU, two of them optical between racks.

4.3 Rail-optimised topologies

The fat tree treats all hosts alike, but GPUs in an AI cluster are not alike: each sits in a server with seven siblings joined by NVLink. Collective libraries such as NCCL exploit this structure hierarchically. An all-reduce across 1,024 GPUs in 128 servers is performed as a reduce-scatter inside each server over NVLink, then an all-reduce across servers between GPUs with the same local index—GPU 0 of every server with GPU 0 of every other, GPU 1 with GPU 1, and so on—and finally an all-gather inside each server (Vol. I §17.6). In the middle phase, GPU \(i\) of one server talks only to GPU \(i\) of other servers.

A rail-optimised topology wires the network to match. The back-end NIC of GPU \(i\) in every server connects to leaf switch \(i\) of its group, called rail \(i\). Figure 4.4 shows the idea for eight-GPU servers: eight rails, each a leaf (or a small group of leaves) collecting the same-index GPUs of many servers. Same-rail traffic—the bulk of a hierarchical collective—crosses only one switch. Traffic between different rails, which occurs when GPU \(i\) of one server must reach GPU \(j\neq i\) of another, can either cross the spine, or first move over NVLink inside the source server to its GPU \(j\) and then travel on rail \(j\), a trick that NCCL and NVIDIA's "PXN" feature perform automatically.

Rail optimisation has three benefits. It shortens the common path to a single hop, lowering latency and the number of links each byte crosses. It concentrates traffic where it can be predicted, so congestion in the spine—the subject of §4.5—affects only the minority of cross-rail traffic. And it opens the door to removing most of the spine. Wang and colleagues analysed the traffic of large-model training in 2023 and found that with rail-optimised placement almost all of it stayed within rails, so a rail-only network, with no spine at all and cross-rail traffic forwarded over NVLink, matched the performance of a full fat tree while cutting network cost by 38–77% and power by 37–75%[2]. Large production clusters, including Meta's 24,000-GPU training clusters, use rail-optimised designs.

The benefit depends on the traffic, and inference traffic is not the same as training traffic. A training job is one large, regular program whose collectives map neatly onto rails. An inference cluster runs many independent replicas, and its cross-server traffic—KV transfers from a prefill server to a decode server, expert-parallel dispatch to whichever GPU hosts an expert, weight streaming from a cache—goes from GPU \(i\) of one server to GPU \(j\) of another, chosen by a scheduler. For such traffic the spine matters, and inference back-ends that support disaggregation or wide expert parallelism need a capable spine or careful rail-aware placement. Whatever the topology, the bytes must be moved by a transport, and the transports used in AI are unlike ordinary TCP.

4.4 Moving bytes without the CPU: RDMA, InfiniBand, RoCE and cloud fabrics

In ordinary networking, sending data from one server's memory to another's involves both operating systems: the sender copies data from the application into kernel buffers, the kernel's TCP stack segments it into packets, the receiving kernel reassembles them and copies them into the receiving application's memory, and both CPUs handle interrupts along the way. At 400 Gb/s per GPU and eight GPUs per server, this would consume many CPU cores just moving bytes, and it would add tens of microseconds of software latency per message. Accelerator networks avoid it with remote direct memory access (RDMA).

For the mathematician — RDMA as a remote write to a registered region

RDMA lets a program on machine A read or write a region of machine B's memory directly through the network interface cards, without involving B's CPU or either operating system on the data path. The model is simple. Each side first registers a memory region with its NIC, which pins it in physical memory and gives it a key; the two sides exchange keys and addresses. Communication then happens through queue pairs: a program posts work requests—"write these bytes from my region at offset \(o_1\) to the remote region with key rkey at offset \(o_2\)"—to a send queue, the NIC executes them in hardware, and completions are reported on a completion queue. A one-sided RDMA_WRITE is, mathematically, an assignment \(B[o_2:o_2+n]\leftarrow A[o_1:o_1+n]\) performed by hardware. With GPUDirect RDMA the registered region can be GPU memory itself, so bytes move from one GPU's HBM through its NIC across the network into another GPU's HBM, touching no CPU and no host memory. This is how NCCL collectives, NIXL KV transfers and DeepEP expert dispatch work.

RDMA needs a network that rarely drops packets, because its hardware transports were designed assuming losses are rare, and recovering from a loss—typically by retransmitting everything after the lost packet—is expensive. Three families of fabric provide it. InfiniBand, standardised in 1999 for high-performance computing and today supplied mainly by NVIDIA (from its acquisition of Mellanox), was designed for RDMA from the start. It is lossless by construction: a link sends a packet only when the receiver has advertised buffer space for it (credit-based flow control), so switches never overflow. A central subnet manager computes routes for the whole fabric, and modern switches support adaptive routing and in-network reductions (SHARP), in which switches themselves sum the data of an all-reduce. InfiniBand is the reference fabric for AI clusters and remains common in neoclouds and on Azure; its drawbacks are a separate ecosystem from Ethernet, vendor concentration, and the need for specialists to operate it.

RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet), in its routable version RoCEv2, carries InfiniBand's transport inside UDP/IP packets over ordinary Ethernet switches. Ethernet drops packets when buffers overflow, so RoCE deployments traditionally made Ethernet lossless with priority flow control (PFC), which lets a switch tell its upstream neighbour to pause a traffic class when buffers fill, combined with end-to-end congestion control such as DCQCN, in which switches mark packets when queues grow and senders reduce their rates in response[3]. PFC has well-known pathologies—pauses propagate backwards and can block unrelated flows (head-of-line blocking), or in rare topologies deadlock—so operating RoCE at scale requires care. Meta described in 2024 how it built and ran RoCE back-ends for clusters of 24,000 GPUs, using a rail-optimised two-stage Clos, careful tuning and collective-aware routing instead of relying on generic congestion control[4]. RoCE's attraction is Ethernet's ecosystem: many switch vendors, common tooling and operational familiarity; NVIDIA's Spectrum-X and similar products add adaptive routing and congestion control tuned for AI.

The hyperscalers built their own transports to fit their networks. AWS's Elastic Fabric Adapter uses a protocol called SRD (Scalable Reliable Datagram), which sprays each flow's packets across many network paths, tolerates their arrival out of order, retransmits lost packets quickly in NIC hardware, and so works over AWS's standard lossy Ethernet fabric without PFC[5]. Google has described its own hardware transports, and in 2025 the Ultra Ethernet Consortium—a group of switch, NIC and cloud companies—published version 1.0 of a specification that standardises similar ideas, including multipath packet spraying and new congestion control, for AI and HPC over Ethernet. The common direction is clear: rather than make Ethernet lossless, make the transport tolerate loss and reordering, and spread traffic over every available path. Why spreading traffic matters so much is the subject of the next section.

4.5 Congestion: why flows collide, and what it costs

A non-blocking fat tree has enough capacity for any traffic pattern in which each host sends and receives at its link rate—if the traffic is spread perfectly over the many equal-cost paths. The standard way of spreading it is equal-cost multi-path routing (ECMP): each switch computes a hash of a packet's flow identifiers (source and destination addresses and ports) and uses it to choose among the equal-cost next hops. All packets of one flow take the same path, which keeps them in order, and different flows are spread pseudo-randomly. For a datacenter full of small, numerous flows this works well, by the law of large numbers. For AI traffic it works badly, and the reason is a classic problem in probability.

Consider one step of a collective in which \(n_f\) flows, each wanting the full link rate \(r\), must cross a tier with \(n_{\text{path}}\) equal-cost paths. ECMP assigns each flow to a path independently and uniformly at random. A path carrying \(c\) flows gives each of them rate \(r/c\). Because a synchronous collective step finishes only when its slowest flow finishes, the step time is proportional to the largest number of flows on any path, \(c_{\max}\), rather than the average \(n_f/n_{\text{path}}\). The efficiency of the step, the ratio of ideal to actual time, is therefore \((n_f/n_{\text{path}})/c_{\max}\). This is the "balls into bins" problem: throw \(n_f\) balls into \(n_{\text{path}}\) bins uniformly at random, and ask for the fullest bin.

Proposition 4.2 — Random hashing of large flows

If \(n_f=n_{\text{path}}=n\) flows are hashed uniformly onto \(n\) paths, then (i) the expected fraction of paths carrying at least one flow is \(1-(1-1/n)^n\to1-1/e\approx0.63\), and (ii) the maximum load satisfies \(c_{\max}=(1+o(1))\ln n/\ln\ln n\) with high probability. Consequently the efficiency of a synchronous step is about \(\ln\ln n/\ln n\), which tends to zero as \(n\) grows.

Proof sketch. (i) A given path is empty with probability \((1-1/n)^n\); linearity of expectation gives the fraction occupied. (ii) The probability that a given path receives at least \(c\) flows is at most \(\binom{n}{c}n^{-c}\le1/c!\); by a union bound over \(n\) paths, \(\Pr(c_{\max}\ge c)\le n/c!\), which is small once \(c!\gg n\), i.e. for \(c\approx\ln n/\ln\ln n\); a matching lower bound follows from the second-moment method. \(\square\)

Proposition 4.2 is sobering. With one large flow per path—exactly the situation of a well-provisioned non-blocking fabric running a collective—random hashing leaves 37% of the paths idle, and the busiest path carries several flows, so the step runs at a fraction of the rate the topology could deliver. Figure 4.5 shows the efficiency from simulation as a function of the number of flows per path, for fabrics with 16, 64 and 256 paths. At one flow per path on 64 paths, efficiency is about 25%: the collective takes four times as long as it should. Only with dozens of flows per path does randomness average out.

Figure 4.5 explains why AI networking has moved away from per-flow hashing, and the remedies follow from the proposition. One remedy is to make flows numerous: collective libraries split each logical transfer over many queue pairs (NCCL can use several per connection), raising \(n_f/n_{\text{path}}\). A second is adaptive routing: switches choose each packet's next hop by current queue depth rather than by a hash, which InfiniBand and AI-oriented Ethernet switches support. A third is packet spraying: send consecutive packets of one flow over different paths and let the receiving NIC reorder them, which is what SRD and Ultra Ethernet do; with spraying, each flow's load is divided evenly over all paths and the balls-into-bins problem disappears. A fourth is avoiding the contended tier: rail-optimised placement keeps most traffic within one switch, where there is no path choice to get wrong. A fifth is scheduling: assigning collective traffic to paths deterministically, as some operators do with source routing or with NCCL's topology awareness.

Congestion also arises without any hashing, from incast: many senders transmitting to one receiver at once, whose link becomes the bottleneck and whose switch buffers overflow. All-to-all exchanges, such as the dispatch and combine of expert parallelism (Vol. I §19.4), produce incast whenever routing sends a disproportionate share of tokens to one GPU's experts. Congestion control—senders slowing down in response to marks or delay—limits the damage but cannot remove the bottleneck; balancing the load at the application level, as expert-load balancers do, is the real fix. For an infrastructure engineer, the lesson of this section is that a network's nominal bisection bandwidth is an upper bound that only good load balancing achieves, and that "the GPUs are slow" is often "the network is congested". Much of that congestion can be avoided by putting the heaviest traffic on links that have no congestion problem at all: those of the scale-up domain.

4.6 Scale-up versus scale-out

An accelerator has two kinds of network port. Its scale-up links—NVLink for NVIDIA, Infinity Fabric for AMD, ICI for TPUs, NeuronLink for Trainium—connect it to a bounded group of peers with very high bandwidth, low latency and memory semantics: a GPU can load and store directly into a peer's memory. Its scale-out port, the back-end NIC, connects it to the whole cluster through the switched network with lower bandwidth and higher latency. Table 4.1 compares them for recent generations.

Every step down the ladder costs roughly an order of magnitude in bandwidth and adds latency, and a well-designed deployment keeps each kind of data movement on the highest rung it can reach: weights and KV in HBM, the per-layer collectives of tensor and expert parallelism on NVLink, per-request transfers on the back-end, bulk loads on the front-end, and only replication and failover across regions.

The ratio in the last column—an order of magnitude—is the most important number in parallel layout. It means that traffic which can stay within a scale-up domain costs about a tenth as much time per byte as traffic that cannot, and has none of the congestion problems of §4.5, since NVSwitch is a true crossbar. Volume I's rule of thumb follows directly: put tensor parallelism, whose all-reduces occur twice per layer per step, inside the scale-up domain; put pipeline parallelism, data parallelism and disaggregated KV transfer, which move fewer bytes less often, across scale-out (Vol. I §18.6). Expert parallelism sits in between: its all-to-all moves a modest number of bytes per token but on every layer, and it is very sensitive to latency and incast. This is why the 72-GPU NVLink domain of GB200 NVL72 was so significant for mixture-of-experts inference: it lets expert parallelism of degree 64 or 72 run entirely on scale-up links.

Worked example — expert parallelism across servers versus inside NVL72

Setup. DeepSeek-V3 decode with expert parallelism of degree 32 on four eight-GPU H100 servers. Each GPU holds a batch of 64 tokens per step. Each token is dispatched to \(k_r=8\) experts as a hidden vector of \(d=7168\) values in FP8 (1 byte), and the experts' outputs are combined back in BF16 (2 bytes). There are 58 MoE layers.

Bytes per GPU per step. Dispatch: \(64\times8\times7168\times1\approx3.7\) MB per layer. Combine: about 7.3 MB per layer. Total about 11 MB per layer, or \(58\times11\approx640\) MB per step.

Across servers. With experts spread evenly, about 24 of every 31 destinations are on other servers, so about 480 MB per GPU per step crosses the back-end at 50 GB/s: about 9.6 ms if load were perfectly balanced over the fabric. At the 25–40% efficiency of hashed flows (Figure 4.5), it would be 24–38 ms—longer than a typical 20–30 ms TPOT budget by itself. This is why DeepSeek overlapped communication with computation using two micro-batches, wrote custom RDMA kernels (DeepEP), and depended on adaptive routing.

Inside an NVL72 rack. The same 640 MB per step on NVLink at 450–900 GB/s per GPU takes about 0.7–1.4 ms, with no hashing and no incast at the switch.

Lesson. Moving wide expert parallelism from scale-out to scale-up reduces its communication time by roughly an order of magnitude, which allows larger batches per GPU within the latency target and is the main reason rack-scale NVLink systems lower the cost per token of large MoE models.

The scale-up domain has grown with each generation—8 GPUs for a decade, then 72 in 2024–2025, with announced designs of 144 and 576 GPU dies—and its growth is limited by physics: copper links work only over short distances at these rates, so the domain must fit in a rack or two, which drives the power densities of Chapter 2. Optics can reach further. Google's TPU v4 used optical circuit switches—mirrors that physically redirect light between fibres—to reconfigure its torus among 4,096 chips, which also lets it route around failed chips[6]. Industry consortia formed in 2024–2025 to define open scale-up standards (UALink, and Ethernet-based scale-up proposals) so that non-NVIDIA accelerators can build large scale-up domains too. Whatever the technology, the infrastructure engineer's question is the same: which traffic of the workload must cross the scale-up boundary, and how much does it cost when it does? For inference, the answer requires a catalogue of the traffic.

The front-end request traffic is negligible in volume: Atlas's 10,000 requests per second at a few kilobytes each, plus streamed tokens of a few dozen bytes each, amount to well under a gigabit per second of payload. The interesting traffic is in the middle: KV transfers for disaggregation and remote prefix caches, and weight loading.

Worked example — the network load of disaggregating Atlas-Max

Setup. Atlas-Max (DeepSeek-V3) runs disaggregated: prefill GPUs compute the KV cache of each prompt and send it to decode GPUs (Vol. I Chapter 13). With MLA, each token's cache is \((d_c+d_h^R)\times L\times b_{kv}=(512+64)\times61\times2\approx70{,}300\) bytes in BF16, about 68.6 KiB (Vol. I §8.4). Each request has 2,000 prompt tokens, and the decode side needs the cache of all of them.

Per request. \(2000\times70{,}300\approx141\) MB, or about 70 MB in FP8.

Aggregate. At 500 requests per second at peak, the prefill pool sends about 70 GB/s (BF16) to the decode pool, or 35 GB/s in FP8.

Per GPU. Spread over 100 prefill GPUs, each sends about 0.7 GB/s (BF16)—about 1.4% of a 50 GB/s NIC. Each of 417 decode GPUs receives about 0.17 GB/s.

Latency. One request's 141 MB over a single 50 GB/s NIC takes about 2.8 ms, against a prefill time for 800 uncached tokens of tens of milliseconds, so the transfer ratio \(\chi\) of Vol. I §13.4 is small and the transfer can be overlapped layer by layer.

Lesson. With MLA's compact cache, KV transfer is a light load. With Llama 3 70B's GQA cache (320 KiB per token in BF16, about 4.7 times larger), the same traffic would be about 330 GB/s, still well within a back-end fabric but no longer negligible on a front-end network—which is why disaggregation runs over the back-end with RDMA (NIXL, Mooncake's transfer engine) rather than over TCP.

Weight loading is the other heavy flow, and it arrives in bursts. When the autoscaler adds replicas, each must fetch its weights: 16 GB for Llama 3 8B in BF16, about 70 GB for Llama 3 70B in FP8, about 700 GB for DeepSeek-V3 in FP8. If fifty new 70B replicas start at once after a traffic spike or a failure, they ask the storage system for 3.5 TB within a minute or two, roughly 30–60 GB/s of aggregate throughput, concentrated on a few objects. Whether that comes over the front-end network from object storage, from local NVMe caches, or peer to peer over the back-end from replicas that already have the weights is the main design question of cold starts, and Chapter 5 develops it. The final network concern is the one every cloud bill makes visible: the front-end network and its costs.

4.8 The front-end network, virtual networks and egress

To the customer, the front-end network of a cloud appears as a virtual private cloud (VPC): a private address space in which instances, load balancers and managed services communicate, isolated from other customers' traffic even though all of it shares the provider's physical switches. Providers implement VPCs with overlays: each packet between instances is encapsulated in an outer packet addressed between physical hosts (VXLAN and Geneve are common encapsulations), and the offload cards of §3.1 perform the encapsulation, apply security-group rules and enforce bandwidth limits in hardware. The overlay is why one customer's addresses can overlap another's and why instances can move between physical hosts without changing address. It is also why the front-end network is oversubscribed and why its bandwidth per instance is capped: it is a shared, multiplexed resource.

Clouds charge for some traffic, and the charges shape architecture. Traffic within an availability zone is usually free; traffic between zones in a region is charged at about a cent per gigabyte in each direction on the major clouds; traffic out to the internet (egress) is charged at several cents per gigabyte, with volume discounts. For an LLM API the egress of streamed responses is small—Atlas's \(2.2\times10^{11}\) output tokens a day at perhaps 50 bytes each including framing are about 11 TB per day, a few hundred dollars—compared with tens of thousands of dollars a day of accelerator time. Cross-zone and cross-region charges matter more for internal flows: replicating 700 GB of weights to every zone, moving KV caches across zones, or shipping logs and traces. A design that routes every request through a load balancer in one zone to replicas in another doubles its internal transfer charges; most providers' guidance is to keep request paths zone-local, which is also good for latency and for limiting failure domains.

Drill 4.2 — Can the front-end carry a cold start?

A replica of Llama 3 70B in FP8 (about 70 GB) starts on an eight-GPU instance whose front-end bandwidth is 200 Gb/s. How long does downloading the weights take at full rate, and at a realistic 60% of it? What if eight such instances start at once and share a 400 Gb/s uplink from their rack?

Solution. 200 Gb/s is 25 GB/s, so 70 GB takes 2.8 s at full rate and about 4.7 s at 60%. Eight instances sharing a 400 Gb/s (50 GB/s) rack uplink fetch 560 GB in about 11 s at full rate, 19 s at 60%—if the storage service can supply 50 GB/s for these objects, which is a large ask of a single bucket and prefix. The network is rarely the binding constraint for one replica; storage throughput and concurrency for many simultaneous replicas usually are, which is why weights are cached locally or distributed peer to peer (Chapter 5).

Looking ahead

We now understand how thousands of accelerators are joined: fat trees and rails in the back-end, RDMA transports that bypass the CPU, the collisions that waste capacity and the remedies for them, the scale-up domain where the heaviest traffic belongs, and the front-end network through which requests arrive. One flow kept appearing at the edge of our analysis: the tens to hundreds of gigabytes of weights that each new replica must load before it can serve a single token. Where do weights live, in what format, how fast can they be read, and how can a fleet start hundreds of replicas in minutes rather than hours? Chapter 5 takes up storage and the anatomy of a cold start.

Chapter summary

  • An AI datacenter has a front-end network (Ethernet, TCP/IP, 100–400 Gb/s per server), a separate back-end network (RDMA, 400–800 Gb/s per GPU), scale-up links inside servers or racks, and an out-of-band management network.
  • Clos/fat-tree networks build large non-blocking switches from small ones: two tiers give \(k_{\text{sw}}^2/2\) hosts with \(3k_{\text{sw}}/2\) switches; three tiers give \(k_{\text{sw}}^3/4\) hosts with \(5k_{\text{sw}}^2/4\) switches. Radix decides the number of tiers.
  • Bisection bandwidth measures capacity across the worst cut; oversubscription is downlink/uplink capacity; back-ends are usually non-blocking; optics dominate cost and failures.
  • Rail-optimised topologies connect GPU \(i\) of every server to rail \(i\), so hierarchical collectives take one hop; rail-only designs drop the spine for training, but inference's any-to-any traffic needs spine capacity or rail-aware placement.
  • RDMA moves bytes NIC-to-NIC (GPUDirect: HBM to HBM) without CPUs. InfiniBand is lossless by credits; RoCEv2 uses PFC plus congestion control (DCQCN) with operational pitfalls; EFA's SRD and Ultra Ethernet spray packets over lossy Ethernet.
  • ECMP hashing of large synchronous flows is a balls-into-bins problem: with one flow per path, about 37% of paths sit idle and efficiency is about 25% on 64 paths. Remedies: more flows, adaptive routing, packet spraying, rail locality, scheduling. Incast needs load balancing at the application.
  • Scale-up bandwidth is about 9–18× scale-out; TP and, ideally, EP belong inside the scale-up domain; NVL72 made 64–72-way expert parallelism possible on NVLink.
  • Inference network loads: KV transfer (light with MLA, heavier with GQA), weight loading in bursts, tiny request traffic. Cloud charges: cross-zone and egress traffic shape architecture more than token streaming does.

Warm-up What are the front-end and back-end networks of a GPU cluster, and why are they separate?

Front-end: ordinary datacenter Ethernet with TCP/IP, one or two NICs per server at 100–400 Gb/s, often oversubscribed; carries API traffic, storage, control plane and internet. Back-end: a separate fabric with one NIC per GPU at 400–800 Gb/s, usually non-blocking, running RDMA (InfiniBand, RoCE, EFA); carries collectives, KV transfers, expert dispatch. Separation gives the GPU traffic dedicated bandwidth, the right transport and congestion behaviour, isolation from bursty general traffic, and a smaller attack surface. Plus scale-up (NVLink) inside servers and an out-of-band management network.

Warm-up What is RDMA and why do AI clusters use it?

Remote direct memory access lets NICs read or write a registered memory region on another machine without involving either CPU or OS on the data path; with GPUDirect RDMA the region can be GPU HBM. Programs post work requests to queue pairs; NIC hardware executes them. It avoids kernel copies, interrupts and protocol processing, delivering line rate (400–800 Gb/s per GPU) with microsecond latency and negligible CPU use. Collectives (NCCL), KV transfer (NIXL, Mooncake) and expert dispatch (DeepEP) depend on it. It needs a network with rare losses (InfiniBand, RoCE with PFC/congestion control) or a loss-tolerant transport (SRD, Ultra Ethernet).

Warm-up InfiniBand or RoCE for a new inference cluster?

InfiniBand: designed for RDMA, lossless by credit flow control, adaptive routing, in-network reductions, mature for AI, centrally managed; but a separate ecosystem, mostly one vendor, specialist operations. RoCEv2: RDMA over Ethernet with many vendors and common tooling, integrates with the rest of the datacenter; but needs PFC and congestion control tuning (head-of-line blocking, deadlocks) or newer loss-tolerant NICs and AI Ethernet switches (Spectrum-X, Ultra Ethernet). At very large scale both work (Meta runs both). Choose by team expertise, vendor strategy, scale, required features, and whether the cloud dictates the fabric (EFA on AWS).

Core Derive the number of hosts and switches in two- and three-tier fat trees, and size a 16k-GPU back-end with 64-port switches.

Two tiers: \(k\) leaves each with \(k/2\) host ports and \(k/2\) uplinks; \(k/2\) spines each with \(k\) ports; \(k^2/2\) hosts, \(3k/2\) switches. Three tiers: \(k\) pods, each \(k/2\) leaves and \(k/2\) aggregation switches (\(k^2/4\) hosts per pod), \(k^2/4\) core switches; \(k^3/4\) hosts, \(5k^2/4\) switches. With \(k=64\): two tiers max 2,048, so three tiers; 16,384 GPUs need 16 pods (1,024 hosts each): 512 leaves and 512 aggregation switches, plus 256 core switches with 64 ports each: about 1,280 switches and about 49,000 links (three per GPU). A rail-optimised variant groups same-index GPUs on the same leaves; a higher-radix switch could reduce the design to two tiers.

Core Explain why ECMP performs poorly for collectives, with a quantitative argument.

ECMP hashes each flow to one of the equal-cost paths. Collectives create few, large, synchronised flows, roughly one per path in a non-blocking fabric. This is balls into bins: with \(n\) flows on \(n\) paths, about \(1/e\) (37%) of paths are empty and the busiest carries about \(\ln n/\ln\ln n\) flows. Since the step ends when the slowest flow finishes, time scales with the maximum load, so efficiency is mean/max—about 25% on 64 paths. Remedies: many flows per transfer (multiple QPs), adaptive routing, packet spraying with receiver reordering (SRD, UEC), rail-optimised placement to avoid path choice, and topology-aware or scheduled routing.

Core What is a rail-optimised topology and when does it help?

Each server's GPU \(i\) NIC connects to leaf group \(i\) (rail \(i\)). Hierarchical collectives (reduce-scatter over NVLink, then same-index all-reduce across servers, then all-gather) keep inter-server traffic within one rail—one switch hop, no spine. Cross-rail traffic can hop over NVLink to the right local GPU first (PXN). Benefits: lower latency, fewer links per byte, less spine congestion, and the option of thin or no spine (rail-only), cutting cost substantially for training. It helps less for inference traffic between arbitrary GPUs (KV transfer, EP across nodes), which needs spine capacity or rail-aware scheduling (e.g. send KV from prefill GPU \(i\) to decode GPU \(i\)).

Core Estimate the network load of disaggregated serving for DeepSeek-V3 and for Llama 3 70B.

KV bytes per prompt token: DeepSeek-V3 with MLA \((512+64)\times61\times2\approx70\) KB BF16; Llama 3 70B with GQA \(2\times80\times8\times128\times2=320\) KiB BF16. For 2,000-token prompts at 500 requests/s: DeepSeek about 70 GB/s total (35 GB/s FP8), Llama about 330 GB/s. Per prefill GPU (100 GPUs) that is 0.7 vs 3.3 GB/s, a few percent of a 50 GB/s NIC; per request transfer time on one NIC 2.8 ms vs 13 ms, small relative to prefill compute and overlappable layer by layer. So both fit easily on a back-end fabric, but GQA models put meaningful load on front-end networks, hence RDMA over the back-end and KV quantisation.

Core Which parallelism should cross the scale-out network and which should not?

Scale-up is about 9–18× faster per GPU and congestion-free. Tensor parallelism (two all-reduces per layer per step, latency-critical) stays within the scale-up domain. Expert parallelism (all-to-all every MoE layer, latency- and incast-sensitive) ideally stays within it (NVL72, TPU pods); across nodes it needs RDMA kernels like DeepEP and careful load balancing. Pipeline parallelism (point-to-point activations between stages, once per micro-batch per stage), data parallelism (no communication at inference), disaggregated KV transfer (once per request, overlappable) and weight loading can cross scale-out. Context parallelism for long prompts can cross scale-out with ring attention overlapping communication with compute.

Deep-dive Prove that with \(n\) flows hashed onto \(n\) paths the maximum load is \(O(\ln n/\ln\ln n)\) and explain its implications.

For a fixed path, \(\Pr(\text{load}\ge c)\le\binom{n}{c}(1/n)^c\le\frac{n^c}{c!}\frac{1}{n^c}=1/c!\). Union bound over \(n\) paths: \(\Pr(c_{\max}\ge c)\le n/c!\). Choose \(c\) with \(c!\ge n^2\): by Stirling, \(c\ln c\approx2\ln n\), so \(c\approx2\ln n/\ln\ln n\) makes the probability at most \(1/n\). A second-moment argument shows the maximum is at least \((1-\epsilon)\ln n/\ln\ln n\) w.h.p. Implications: efficiency \(\approx\ln\ln n/\ln n\to0\) as fabrics grow, so bigger non-blocking networks waste more of their capacity under per-flow hashing; balancing must be done per packet or adaptively. The "power of two choices" result (choose the less loaded of two random paths) reduces max load to \(\ln\ln n/\ln2+O(1)\), which is the theory behind adaptive and flowlet routing.

Deep-dive Why did hyperscalers and the Ultra Ethernet Consortium move towards loss-tolerant, multipath transports rather than lossless Ethernet?

Lossless Ethernet with PFC brings head-of-line blocking, pause storms, congestion spreading and deadlock risks, and requires delicate tuning; and classic RDMA transports (go-back-N) recover badly from loss, so they depend on losslessness. Meanwhile per-flow ECMP wastes capacity for elephant flows. A transport that sprays packets across all paths, accepts out-of-order delivery (with NIC reorder buffers or direct placement), uses selective retransmission in hardware and modern congestion control solves both: load balances evenly (no balls-into-bins problem) and tolerates the occasional drop on standard switches. AWS's SRD demonstrated this at scale; UEC 1.0 (2025) standardises it so that multiple vendors' NICs and switches interoperate.

Deep-dive Users report that a multi-node inference deployment is "randomly slow". How do you determine whether the network is at fault?

Correlate per-step latencies with communication phases: instrument engine steps (time in all-to-all or KV transfer vs compute), collect NCCL/NIXL timing and NIC counters (retransmits, out-of-order packets, PFC pause frames, ECN marks, congestion drops), switch telemetry (queue depths, link utilisation, errors, flaps), and per-link optical diagnostics. Test pairwise bandwidth and latency between affected nodes with perftest or NCCL tests, compare with healthy pairs. Look for patterns: specific links or switches (bad optics, CRC errors), specific times (other jobs creating incast or hash collisions), specific GPUs (expert hotspots causing incast), or topology (cross-rail traffic crossing an oversubscribed spine). Fixes range from replacing optics and draining bad links, to rebalancing experts, enabling adaptive routing or spraying, and rail-aware placement.

Design Design the network for a 4,096-GPU inference cluster that will run disaggregated serving of DeepSeek-V3 and many smaller replicas.

Scale-up: prefer NVL72 racks (57 racks) so DeepSeek-V3 decode uses wide EP inside one NVLink domain; smaller models fit within a server or rack. Back-end: one 400–800 Gb/s NIC per GPU, rail-optimised, non-blocking two-tier if radix allows (128-port 400G: 8,192 hosts) else three-tier; adaptive routing or packet spraying for any-to-any KV traffic; RDMA (NIXL) for prefill→decode transfers and remote prefix caches; rail-aware placement of prefill and decode pairs. Front-end: 200–400 Gb/s per server for weight downloads and requests, zone-local load balancers. Storage: local NVMe weight caches, peer-to-peer weight distribution over the back-end. Operations: telemetry on NICs and switches, optics monitoring, automatic link draining, failure domains aligned with racks and switch groups.

Design How would you reduce network cost for a training-and-inference cluster without hurting performance?

Use the highest-radix switches available to cut tiers; rail-optimised design so most training traffic avoids the spine; thin the spine (oversubscribe cross-rail) if measurements show little cross-rail traffic, keeping enough for inference KV and EP traffic, or schedule inference pairs on the same rails; put the heaviest traffic (TP, EP) on scale-up domains (NVL72); use copper (DACs) within racks and optics only where needed; consider linear-drive or co-packaged optics to cut transceiver power; share one back-end between training and inference with QoS; and keep the front-end oversubscribed as it serves general traffic. Validate with traffic matrices from real workloads rather than worst-case assumptions.

Core What drives cloud network charges for an LLM service, and how do you design to minimise them?

Intra-zone traffic is usually free; inter-zone is about a cent per GB each way; egress to the internet several cents per GB; inter-region charges vary. Token streams are small (≈ tens of bytes per token), so egress for responses is minor. Large internal flows dominate: weight replication across zones/regions, KV or prefix-cache movement across zones, logs/traces/telemetry, dataset and checkpoint copies, cross-zone load balancing that sends requests to replicas in other zones. Minimise by zone-local request paths and caches, replicating weights once per zone into local caches, compressing and sampling telemetry, using private interconnects for cross-region flows, and tracking network cost per million tokens as a FinOps metric.

Deep-dive Explain incast and how it shows up in MoE inference.

Incast is many senders transmitting to one receiver simultaneously; the receiver's link (and the last switch's buffers) becomes the bottleneck, causing queueing, ECN marks, pauses or drops, and long tail latency. In expert-parallel all-to-all, each GPU sends the tokens routed to experts on every other GPU. If routing is unbalanced—popular experts, correlated prompts—some GPUs receive far more tokens than average, so their inbound links congest while others idle, and the whole step waits for them. Mitigations: auxiliary-loss-free or bias-based load balancing during training, expert replication and placement (EPLB) at serving time, capacity factors with token dropping (rarely used at inference), topology-aware dispatch kernels (DeepEP's hierarchical NVLink-then-RDMA scheme), and keeping EP within NVLink domains.

Core Explain bisection bandwidth and oversubscription, and why AI back-ends are usually built non-blocking.

Bisection bandwidth is the minimum total capacity across any cut splitting the hosts into equal halves; for a non-blocking fat tree it is \(Hr/2\). Oversubscription is the ratio of downlink to uplink capacity at a tier; 3:1 means hosts under a leaf can send only a third of their capacity outward simultaneously. General traffic rarely saturates all hosts at once, so front-ends are oversubscribed to save cost. Collectives drive every GPU at line rate simultaneously and synchronously, so oversubscription directly lengthens every step, hence 1:1 back-ends—though rail-optimised designs can oversubscribe the spine when most traffic stays within rails.
