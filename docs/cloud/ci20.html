Volume II · Part VI · Synthesis — Chapter 20

The Cloud Infrastructure Design Interview

An infrastructure interview does not ask for chapters. It asks for a system—"design a global LLM API", "design a serverless GPU platform", "design the scheduler for our cluster"—and gives you forty-five minutes, a whiteboard and an interviewer who will push on whichever part you understand least. This final chapter turns the volume into that performance: a method for structuring the answer, a toolkit of numbers and formulas to estimate with, five worked cases, and the criteria by which answers are judged.

Why this chapter

Volume I ended with the model-serving design interview: given a model and a latency target, choose the engine configuration, the batch size, the parallelism and the cache policy (Vol. I Chapter 30). The questions of this volume sit one level up. The model and the engine are taken as components, and the candidate is asked to design what surrounds them: fleets across regions, capacity and its cost, schedulers, platforms that other teams build on, and the behaviour of the whole when something fails. These are the questions asked for infrastructure, platform and site-reliability roles at AI laboratories, at cloud providers and at any company that runs models at scale, and at senior levels they are usually the deciding rounds.

The difficulty is rarely knowledge of any one topic. It is assembly under time pressure: deciding what to ask, estimating before designing, drawing a system whose parts connect, going deep where it matters, and saying what would break. Candidates who know every chapter of this book still fail by designing without numbers, by describing components instead of making decisions, or by never mentioning failure. A method prevents all three, and practice on realistic cases makes it automatic.

We describe what the interview assesses and a method that fits it (§20.1), then collect the estimation toolkit (§20.2). Three full cases follow: a global LLM API (§20.3), a serverless GPU platform (§20.4) and a scheduler for a ten-thousand-GPU cluster (§20.5); then two shorter ones, a billion-vector retrieval service and an agent sandbox fleet (§20.6). We end with how answers are graded and the mistakes that sink them (§20.7).

Learning objectives
  1. Structure a 45-minute infrastructure design answer: requirements, estimates, architecture, deep dives, failures, trade-offs.
  2. Convert a demand statement into GPUs, megawatts, dollars and replicas from memory, using a small set of constants and formulas.
  3. Design a multi-region LLM API with capacity, routing, reliability and cost reasoning.
  4. Design a serverless GPU platform, quantifying pooling gains, cold starts and keep-warm policies.
  5. Design a cluster scheduler for mixed inference, training and batch workloads.
  6. Recognise what distinguishes senior and staff-level answers, and avoid the common failure modes.

Uses: every earlier chapter of this volume, and the engine-level design method of Vol. I Chapter 30.

20.1 What the interview assesses, and a method that fits

An interviewer in a design round is trying to predict how you would behave as the owner of a real system, and looks for five things. Scoping: do you find out what is being asked—scale, latency targets, tenancy, regions, budget—before designing, and do you choose sensible assumptions when the interviewer declines to answer? Quantitative reasoning: can you estimate the size of the system and let the numbers drive the design? Architecture: do the components connect into something that would work, with clear responsibilities and interfaces? Depth: when pushed on one part, do you know how it really works and what its failure modes are? Judgement: do you see the trade-offs, state what you would choose and why, and know what you would measure to find out whether you were right? Knowledge of buzzwords is not on the list, and naming a product is never an answer to a design question.

A method makes room for all five in the time available. Figure 20.1 shows one allocation of a 45-minute round. The proportions matter more than the minutes: a third of the time on scoping and estimation feels extravagant to candidates and is exactly what interviewers reward, because the estimates determine which parts of the design are hard.

Figure 20.1's six steps deserve a sentence each. In scope, ask who the users are, what the load is (requests per second at peak, tokens in and out), what the latency and availability targets are, which models, how many regions, and what is out of scope; write the answers down as a requirements list. In estimate, turn that load into GPUs, power and money (§20.2), and notice what dominates. In architecture, draw the request path from client to GPU and back, then the control plane that manages it (§1.7), naming each component's job. In deep dives, pick the two parts the numbers make hardest—or accept the interviewer's choice—and reason about them at the level of mechanisms. In failures, walk through the loss of a node, a zone, a region and a dependency, and say what users experience. In wrap-up, summarise the main trade-offs, what you would build first, and what you would monitor. Speak in decisions: not "we could use a warm pool" but "I would keep a warm pool of ten replicas, because a cold start is 70 seconds and the burst rate needs that many within it".

20.2 The estimation toolkit

Estimation in an interview is done from memory, so it rests on a small set of constants and a smaller set of formulas. The constants below are those this volume has used throughout; rounding them is fine, but being wrong by a factor of ten is not, and interviewers notice both fluency and sanity checks.

Hardware. An H100 has 80 GB of memory, 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth and about \(10^{15}\) FLOP/s of dense BF16 arithmetic; all-in it draws about 1.4 kW of IT power and rents for about $2.50 per hour on a multi-year commitment, so a GPU-year costs about $22,000 and a megawatt of IT power holds about 700 of them (§1.6). Newer generations roughly double memory and throughput per GPU at about 2 kW. Facility power is IT power times a PUE of 1.1–1.5 (Chapter 2). A 400 Gb/s link moves 50 GB/s; a local NVMe drive reads a few GB/s; object storage delivers 1–2 GB/s per stream (Chapters 4 and 5).

Models. Llama 3 8B is 16 GB in BF16 with 128 KiB of KV cache per token; Llama 3 70B is 141 GB with 320 KiB per token, and is served across eight H100s. Online, a single H100 produces about 9,000 output tokens per second from the 8B model, and an eight-GPU replica about 6,000 from the 70B model—so the larger model costs roughly twelve times as much per token. Prefill costs \(2N\) FLOPs per token; decode is bandwidth-bound (Vol. I Chapter 6).

Demand. Peak-to-mean ratios of 1.5–2 are typical for regional traffic; fleets sized for the peak with headroom average about 55% utilisation (§1.6, §9.3). The basic conversion is

\[ n_{\text{gpu}}=\frac{\lambda_{\text{peak}}\;n_{\text{out}}}{r_{\text{gpu}}\;u_{\text{peak}}}, \]

where \(\lambda_{\text{peak}}\) is the peak request rate, \(n_{\text{out}}\) the mean output tokens, \(r_{\text{gpu}}\) the output tokens per second one GPU produces at its operating point (with prefill included in the measured rate), and \(u_{\text{peak}}\) the utilisation allowed at peak, typically 0.75. Figure 20.2 plots it for the two dense running models.

Figure 20.2 is the first thing to reproduce on a whiteboard, because it settles the character of the system: 600 GPUs is one cluster in one hall; 7,000 is several megawatts and a procurement programme. From GPUs the rest follows by multiplication—power at 1.4 kW each, cost at $22,000 a year each, replicas at one or eight GPUs each—and from the cost and the daily token volume, the cost per million tokens (§10.3). Atlas, the volume's running example, is the mixed case: 10,000 requests per second at peak, split 60/35/5 between an 8B tier, a 70B tier and a DeepSeek-V3 tier, on about 4,000 H100s drawing 5.6 MW and costing about $88 million a year.

Formulas worth carrying. Little's law, \(\text{concurrency}=\lambda\times\text{time in system}\), sizes anything with a rate and a duration: sandboxes, reviewers, connections, KV memory. Availability: 99.9% allows 43.8 minutes of downtime a month, 99.99% allows 4.4; components in series multiply availabilities, and \(k\) independent replicas in parallel fail together with probability \(p^k\) (§13.2). Regional redundancy: to survive the loss of one of \(r\) regions at peak, provision \(r/(r-1)\) times the peak (§12.4). Cold starts: time \(\approx\) bytes over bandwidth plus initialisation (§5.1). Pooling: the sum of \(n\) independent demands has a standard deviation that grows as \(\sqrt n\), so headroom per unit shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\) (§2.4). Compounding: a chain of \(n\) steps each succeeding with probability \(p\) succeeds with \(p^n\) (§17.7). Precision at low prevalence (§18.3). With these and the constants above, every case in this chapter can be sized in two minutes.

Drill 20.1 — Two-minute sizing

A product expects 3,000 requests per second at peak on a 70B-class model, with 500 output tokens per request and a peak-to-mean ratio of 1.8. Estimate the GPUs, IT power, annual hardware cost, and cost per million output tokens.

Solution. \(n_{\text{gpu}}=3000\times500/(750\times0.75)\approx2{,}670\) H100s, about 330 eight-GPU replicas. Power: \(2{,}670\times1.4\approx3.7\) MW of IT load. Cost: \(2{,}670\times\$22{,}000\approx\$59\) million a year. Mean rate: \(3000/1.8\approx1{,}670\) requests per second, \(8.3\times10^5\) output tokens per second, \(2.6\times10^{13}\) per year; \(\$59\times10^6/2.6\times10^{7}\) million tokens \(\approx\$2.25\) per million output tokens of hardware cost, before electricity, staff and margin. Sanity check: mean utilisation is \(0.75/1.8\approx42\%\), on the low side, so a batch tier or a second time zone would improve the economics.

20.3 Case study: a global LLM API

"Design a public LLM API serving three model tiers to customers on three continents." This is the Atlas problem, and the whole volume is its answer; the interview skill is compressing it.

Scope. Ask, or assume and state: 10,000 requests per second at global peak; three tiers (small, large, reasoning); streaming responses; TTFT under 500 ms at the 95th percentile for the small and large tiers; 99.9% availability; enterprise customers with data-residency requirements in Europe; token-priced, with rate limits per customer. Out of scope: training, the engine's internals.

Estimate. By §20.2, about 4,000 H100s: roughly 600 for the 8B tier, 2,500 for the 70B tier and 900 for the reasoning tier; 5.6 MW of IT load; $88 million a year. The peak in each region is offset by time zone, so three regions each carry about 40% of the global peak at their own peak. To survive the loss of one region the survivors must absorb its traffic: with three regions, provision \(3/2\) of what each would otherwise need, unless the business accepts shedding the batch tier and degrading the reasoning tier in a regional outage—a choice worth saying aloud, because it is the difference between 4,000 and 6,000 GPUs.

Architecture. Figure 20.3 is the picture to draw, in this order. The request path: global routing sends each client to the nearest region allowed by its residency policy (Chapter 12); the gateway authenticates, applies token-aware rate limits, starts metering, runs the input guard in parallel with prefill and holds the stream until its verdict (Chapters 11 and 18); the inference router picks a replica by prefix-cache affinity and load, and applies admission control (Chapter 8); the engine streams tokens back through the gateway. The regional control plane: Kubernetes with a GPU-aware scheduler places replicas across zones; an autoscaler follows the diurnal curve with a warm pool for bursts; weights are cached on local disks and distributed peer to peer (Chapters 5–7 and 9). The global control plane: capacity planning and placement of models across regions, staged rollout of models and configuration, quotas and billing, and the SLO dashboards.

Deep dives. The numbers suggest two. First, capacity and cost: commit to roughly the trough-to-mean level, cover the diurnal swing with shorter commitments and autoscaling, sell the trough as a batch tier at half price (§19.3), and place the reasoning tier in the regions with cheapest power, since its users tolerate latency. Second, overload behaviour: when demand exceeds capacity the system must degrade in a planned order—pause batch, queue then shed the lowest-priority tenants with a retry-after header, protect goodput with admission control rather than letting every request miss its deadline (§11.6)—and retries must be budgeted so that a brief overload does not become a retry storm.

Failures. Node loss: the router's health checks eject the replica within seconds, in-flight streams fail and clients retry with idempotency keys; a few replicas of headroom absorb it. Zone loss: a third of a region's capacity, covered by spreading replicas across zones and by overflow to other regions for traffic without residency constraints. Region loss: global routing withdraws it; the others absorb what their headroom allows and shed the rest by tier. Control-plane loss: the data plane must keep serving with its last configuration—static stability (§13.6). A bad rollout is the most likely outage of all, hence canaries by region and automatic rollback.

20.4 Case study: a serverless GPU platform

"Design a platform where customers deploy their own models and pay only while requests are running." The product promise—scale to zero, pay per second—is economically possible only because of statistical pooling, and technically hard only because of cold starts. A good answer makes both quantitative.

Scope. Customers upload a container or name a model; the platform gives them an endpoint; replicas start on demand and stop when idle; billing is per second of GPU time. Assume 10,000 deployed endpoints, most of them rarely used, models from 2 GB to 150 GB, a target first-response time under 10 s for small models on a cold start, and strong isolation between customers.

Worked example — why scale-to-zero can be sold at all

One customer. An endpoint is busy 6% of the time. A dedicated H100 at $2.50 per hour costs $60 a day; on a serverless platform charging $5.00 per active GPU-hour it costs \(0.06\times24\times5.00=\$7.20\). The customer saves a factor of eight despite paying double the hourly rate. The break-even utilisation is \(2.50/5.00=50\%\): above it, dedicated capacity is cheaper.

The platform. Suppose 1,000 such endpoints, each independently active with probability 0.06. The number active at once has mean 60 and standard deviation \(\sqrt{1000\times0.06\times0.94}\approx7.5\). Provisioning for the mean plus three standard deviations needs about 83 GPUs—not 1,000. Revenue is \(60\times\$5.00=\$300\) per hour against a cost of \(83\times\$2.50\approx\$208\): a margin of about 30%, from GPUs that are 72% utilised.

The catch. Demands are not independent—customers share a working day—so the peak is higher than the binomial says; and each activation must load a model onto a GPU that just held another customer's. The margin is spent on the peak-hour headroom and on making that switch fast.

Architecture. Figure 20.4 shows the parts. The front door knows, for every endpoint, which replicas are live. If one is, the request is routed to it; if none is, an activator holds the request and asks the autoscaler for a replica. The placer chooses a node—preferring one whose local disk already holds that model's weights and image—and starts the replica inside an isolation boundary, a microVM or a hardened container with a dedicated GPU (§14.2, §17.3). When the replica reports ready, the buffered request is released. A replica idle for longer than a keep-warm timeout is stopped; its weights stay in the node's cache until evicted.

Deep dive: the cold start. By §5.1, the cold start is a sum: acquire a node (minutes if one must be booted, hence a warm pool of booted nodes), pull the image (seconds if cached, minutes if not), load weights (bytes over bandwidth: 16 GB at a few GB/s from local NVMe is under 10 s; 141 GB from object storage at 2 GB/s is over a minute), and initialise the engine. The platform's engineering is a campaign against each term: lazy-loading image formats, streaming weights straight into GPU memory, peer-to-peer transfer from nodes that hold them, placement by cache affinity, and snapshots of an initialised process restored in seconds. What remains is managed by policy, the keep-warm timeout. If an endpoint's requests arrive as a Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\), a request finds the replica gone exactly when the gap since the previous request exceeded the timeout \(\tau_{\text{warm}}\), which happens with probability \(e^{-\lambda\tau_{\text{warm}}}\). Figure 20.5 plots it.

Figure 20.5 explains serverless pricing and product design. For endpoints with steady traffic a short timeout suffices, and the idle time it adds is small. For rarely used endpoints no affordable timeout helps, so the product must either make the cold start itself fast, or let the customer pay for a minimum of one always-on replica, or set expectations with an asynchronous API. The timeout is a cost to someone: idle-but-warm seconds are either billed to the customer or absorbed by the platform, and platforms differ mainly in who pays. An adaptive policy sets each endpoint's timeout from its own arrival history, long where gaps are short and regular, zero where they are not.

Second deep dive: multi-tenancy. Customers run arbitrary containers, so the isolation of Chapter 14 is not optional: a virtual machine or microVM boundary around each replica, GPUs assigned whole (or as MIG slices) and scrubbed between tenants, no shared host paths, network policy that denies traffic between tenants, and per-tenant quotas so that one customer's burst cannot exhaust the warm pool. Failures: a node dies—replicas restart elsewhere, cold; the registry is unreachable—cached models still start, new ones cannot, so the cache is also a reliability feature; a regional capacity shortage—queue activations with honest latency estimates and reject beyond a bound, rather than time out silently.

Drill 20.2 — Choosing a keep-warm timeout

An endpoint receives Poisson requests at one per 4 minutes; each takes 20 s of GPU time; a cold start takes 40 s. The platform bills active time only, at $5.00 per GPU-hour, and its own cost is $2.50 per GPU-hour whenever a GPU is held. With a timeout of 8 minutes, what fraction of requests start cold, and roughly what fraction of held GPU time is billable? Is the endpoint profitable for the platform?

Solution. \(\lambda\tau_{\text{warm}}=8/4=2\), so cold starts are \(e^{-2}\approx13.5\%\) of requests. Per request the GPU is held for 20 s of work plus the idle wait until the next request or the timeout, whose mean is \((1-e^{-\lambda\tau_{\text{warm}}})/\lambda=0.865\times240\approx208\) s, plus 40 s of loading on the 13.5% that start cold (5 s on average): about 233 s held, of which 20 s is billable, under 9%. Revenue per request is \(20/3600\times5.00\approx\$0.028\); cost is \(233/3600\times2.50\approx\$0.16\). The endpoint loses money at this timeout. A timeout near zero with a fast cold start, or billing for warm idle time, is required—which is why platforms charge for "provisioned" warmth.

20.5 Case study: a scheduler for a ten-thousand-GPU cluster

"Design the scheduler for a cluster of 10,000 GPUs shared by inference services, training jobs and batch work." This case tests whether a candidate understands that scheduling GPUs is a packing problem with topology, priorities and time, not a queue.

Scope. 10,000 GPUs are 1,250 eight-GPU nodes in perhaps 40 racks behind a fat-tree network (Chapter 4). Workloads: inference replicas needing 1–8 GPUs on one node, some needing 16–64 across adjacent nodes, with scale-up decisions needed in seconds; training jobs needing 64–2,048 GPUs all at once (gangs) with fast interconnect, running for days; batch and rollout jobs that are flexible and preemptible. Several teams share the cluster under quotas. Targets: more than 90% of GPUs allocated, inference scale-ups placed in under a second, no team starved.

Architecture. Figure 20.6 separates four decisions that beginners merge. Admission: is this workload entitled to run now—does its team have quota, guaranteed or borrowed, and for a gang, can all of it fit? Placement: on which GPUs? Nodes are filtered by hard constraints and scored by soft ones: keep a replica's GPUs on one node, a gang's nodes under one switch, prefer the node that leaves the least unusable remainder, prefer nodes that already cache the weights, and spread a service's replicas across racks and zones. Preemption: when a high-priority workload does not fit, which lower-priority ones are evicted, choosing the fewest and cheapest victims. Defragmentation: over time, small allocations leave every node partly used, so that 2,000 GPUs may be free with no node entirely free; the scheduler must migrate or preempt to consolidate (Chapter 7).

Worked example — the price of fragmentation

State. The cluster is 80% allocated: 2,000 of 10,000 GPUs are free, but spread evenly, about 1.6 free GPUs on each of 1,250 nodes. No node is entirely free.

Request. A training job asks for 512 GPUs as 64 whole nodes under one group of switches. Although four times that many GPUs are free, the job cannot start: the largest placeable gang is zero nodes.

Defragment. Choose 64 adjacent nodes and move their workloads elsewhere. They hold \(64\times(8-1.6)\approx410\) GPUs of work; the other 1,186 nodes have about 1,900 free GPUs, so it fits. If the displaced work is inference replicas that each take two minutes to restart elsewhere (started before the old ones are drained), the cost is about \(410\times2/60\approx14\) GPU-hours, some $35.

Do nothing. The gang waits. Each hour of delay idles nothing visibly—the free GPUs were idle anyway—but 2,000 free GPUs that can host no large job are worth \(2{,}000\times\$2.50=\$5{,}000\) per hour of stranded capacity.

Lesson. Report "largest gang placeable now", not "GPUs free". Best-fit placement keeps the number from decaying, and cheap, routine migration of small workloads restores it.

Deep dive: large gangs versus utilisation. A 2,048-GPU training job needs 256 whole nodes at once. Waiting for them to become free by chance never succeeds in a busy cluster, and holding nodes idle while they accumulate wastes capacity. The standard answer is a reservation with backfill: the scheduler computes the earliest time the gang can start, given running jobs' expected ends and what it may preempt, reserves those nodes for that time, and meanwhile fills them with batch and rollout work that is guaranteed to be preemptible by then. The preemptible classes are thus what makes large gangs compatible with 90% utilisation—an example of flexible work as the lubricant of a cluster, as in Chapter 19.

Deep dive: inference inside a batch scheduler. Inference scale-ups must be placed in under a second, while optimising gang placement can take many seconds. Give inference its own fast path: a priority class above everything preemptible, a small pool of free or instantly preemptible GPUs kept as headroom, placement that only filters and picks the best of a sample of nodes, and optimistic concurrency against the shared state so that the fast path and the slow path do not block each other. Scale-downs are as important: an inference replica being removed should drain its streams first (§13.5).

Failures. A node fails—its workloads are requeued; inference replicas elsewhere absorb the load and the autoscaler replaces them; a gang job restarts from its checkpoint (§7.5), which is why node health prediction and draining ahead of failure pay. A switch fails—a rack disappears, so replicas of one service must never share a rack. The scheduler itself fails—running workloads must be unaffected, with a standby taking over from the replicated store. And the slow failures: quota that no longer matches the organisation, and fragmentation that creeps up until a dashboard of "largest gang placeable now" makes it visible.

20.6 Two shorter cases

A retrieval service over a billion documents. Scope: one billion chunks, 1,024-dimensional embeddings, 2,000 queries per second, recall above 0.95 at ten results, 50 ms at the 99th percentile, with continuous ingestion. Estimate: raw vectors are \(10^9\times1024\times4\approx4\) TB—far beyond one machine's memory, which decides the design. Product quantisation to 64 bytes per vector makes the codes 64 GB; a graph index over the full vectors would need terabytes of RAM. Design: shard the corpus across nodes; on each, an inverted-file index with product-quantised codes in memory and full vectors on SSD for re-ranking, or a disk-based graph index; fan out each query to all shards (or route to a subset by a coarse quantiser) and merge. Embedding the queries is cheap; embedding the corpus is a batch job of the kind in Chapter 19—a billion chunks of 300 tokens on a 0.3B encoder is a few hundred GPU-hours. Deep dives: the recall–latency trade through the number of lists probed and the re-ranking depth (Chapter 16); freshness, by writing new vectors to a small mutable index merged periodically; filtering by tenant and permissions, which must happen inside the search, not after it. Failures: replicate every shard; a missing shard silently lowers recall, so report partial results explicitly.

A sandbox fleet for coding agents. Scope: 50,000 concurrent agent sessions, each with a private sandbox for running model-written code; sessions last about 12 minutes; start time under a second; no escape, no cross-tenant access, no uncontrolled network egress. Estimate: by Little's law, 50,000 live sandboxes; at 2 vCPUs and 4 GiB each, 100,000 vCPUs and 200 TiB if reserved, but sandboxes are busy about a fifth of the time, so overcommit CPU fourfold—about 130 large servers—and snapshot idle sandboxes to disk (§17.3). Creation rate is about 70 per second. Design: microVMs restored from snapshots of prepared images; a placement service packing them onto hosts; per-sandbox network policy with an allow-listed proxy for package mirrors; a file service for each session's workspace; an API the agent orchestrator calls to execute commands; durable session records so that a lost host loses at most the current step (§17.7). Deep dives: the isolation boundary and why containers alone are insufficient; the idle policy that trades memory against resume latency—and against the KV cache the model holds while the tool runs (§17.4). Failures: host loss—restore from the last snapshot; a noisy neighbour—cgroup limits and eviction; abuse such as mining or scanning—egress policy and anomaly detection.

The two cases look unrelated, and the method is identical: one estimate decides the architecture (4 TB does not fit in memory; 50,000 sandboxes are a CPU fleet), the design is a request path plus a control plane, and the deep dives are the places where the numbers are tight.

20.7 How answers are graded, and how they fail

Interviewers grade against the five qualities of §20.1, and the difference between levels is consistent across companies. A mid-level answer produces a reasonable architecture when led and knows the components. A senior answer drives itself: it scopes without prompting, estimates, makes decisions with reasons, and handles the failure discussion competently. A staff-level answer adds three things: it identifies which requirement is really the hard one and reshapes the problem around it ("the expensive question here is whether we must survive a region loss without shedding load"); it reasons about cost and organisation as fluently as about technology—what to build first, what to buy, what the system will cost per unit of what it serves; and it knows the second-order effects—what the design does to the on-call engineer, to the capacity plan, to the next model twice as large.

The failure modes are equally consistent, and all are avoidable. No numbers: a design that would be the same for a hundred requests per second and a hundred thousand is not a design. Sizing for the mean: forgetting the peak-to-mean ratio, headroom and redundancy, and so underestimating by a factor of two to three. A component tour: listing a gateway, a queue, a cache and a database without saying what each decides and why it is there. Ignoring the control plane: drawing only the request path, with nothing about how replicas come to exist, how models are rolled out, or how configuration changes. No failure story: never saying what happens when a node, a zone or a dependency disappears. Forgetting cold starts: assuming capacity appears the instant an autoscaler asks. Treating GPUs as CPUs: forgetting that they are scarce, that replicas take minutes to start and gigabytes to load, that memory rather than compute often limits them, and that a single model can need eight of them at once. Silence about cost, in a field where the hardware bill dominates. And refusing to choose: presenting three options and stopping. State the choice, the reason and what would change your mind.

Preparation follows from this. Practise the estimates until the constants of §20.2 are reflexes. Practise aloud, with a timer, on the cases of this chapter and on variations—halve the latency target, add a region, replace the model with one ten times larger—because variations are what interviewers use to find the edge of understanding. For each chapter of this volume, be able to give the two-minute version: the bottleneck, the idea, the formula, one number. The questions at the end of every chapter are that practice, and the progress page shows which ones to revisit.

Looking back, and ahead

This volume began with a single GPU that could serve a hundred people and asked what it takes to serve a planet. The answer filled twenty chapters, but its shape is simple. Physical limits come first: power, cooling, memory bandwidth, the speed of a network link. Above them sit the systems that allocate scarce accelerators to work—schedulers, platforms, autoscalers—and the economics that decide what is worth building. Around them are the properties that make a service trustworthy: reliability, security, guardrails. And on top is an ever-widening set of workloads, from chat to agents to the rollouts that train the next model. At every level the method was the same one Volume I used for a single forward pass: find the bottleneck, write its formula, put numbers in, and let the numbers choose the design.

The field will not stand still. Accelerators will change, racks will draw more power than today's whole rows, models will grow and their workloads will take new forms; some numbers in this book will be out of date within a year. The reasoning will not be. An engineer who can derive the cost of a token from the price of a kilowatt-hour, who knows why a cold start takes as long as it does, and who asks of every design what happens when a part of it fails, will be able to read whatever comes next—and to help build it.

Chapter summary

  • Design interviews assess scoping, quantitative reasoning, architecture, depth and judgement. Use six steps—scope, estimate, architecture, two deep dives, failures, wrap-up—and speak in decisions with reasons.
  • Carry the constants: H100 = 80 GB, 3.35 TB/s, ≈ 1.4 kW all-in, ≈ $22k a year; 8B ≈ 9,000 tokens/s per GPU, 70B ≈ 6,000 per eight-GPU replica; peak-to-mean ≈ 1.8; mean utilisation ≈ 55%. Fleet size is \(\lambda_{\text{peak}}n_{\text{out}}/(r_{\text{gpu}}u_{\text{peak}})\).
  • Carry the formulas: Little's law, availability arithmetic, \(r/(r-1)\) regional redundancy, bytes-over-bandwidth cold starts, \(\sqrt n\) pooling, \(p^n\) compounding, precision at low prevalence.
  • Global LLM API: route by latency and residency; gateway (auth, limits, metering, guards) → router (cache- and load-aware, admission) → per-model pools; regional and global control planes; deep dives on capacity/cost and overload; failures by node, zone, region, rollout.
  • Serverless GPU platform: pooling makes scale-to-zero profitable (1,000 endpoints at 6% need ≈ 83 GPUs); cold starts are the engineering problem; cold-start probability \(e^{-\lambda\tau_{\text{warm}}}\) shows timeouts help busy endpoints only; isolation between tenants is mandatory.
  • Cluster scheduler: separate admission, placement, preemption and defragmentation; topology- and fragmentation-aware scoring; reservations with backfill for large gangs; a fast path for inference; requeue on failure.
  • Retrieval and sandbox fleets follow the same method: one estimate (4 TB of vectors; 50,000 sandboxes) decides the architecture.

Design Design a scheduler for a 10,000-GPU cluster shared by inference, training and batch.

Classes and priorities: inference > training > batch/rollouts (preemptible). Queues with per-team guaranteed quota and borrowing; gang admission (all-or-nothing). Placement: filter (GPU type, health, zone), score (same node → rack → pod, best-fit to limit fragmentation, cached weights, spread across failure domains). Preemption: lowest priority, fewest victims, graceful. Reservations + backfill for large gangs. Defragmentation by migration/preemption of flexible work. Inference fast path with headroom pool and sub-second placement; optimistic concurrency on replicated state. Failure handling: requeue, checkpoint restart, drain on predicted failure. Metrics: allocation %, largest placeable gang, queue wait by class, preemption rate.

Core How do you choose a keep-warm timeout for a serverless endpoint?

For Poisson arrivals, P(cold) = e^(−λτ); expected idle held per request = (1 − e^(−λτ))/λ. Choose τ to minimise idle cost + cold-start penalty × P(cold), per endpoint from its inter-arrival history (real traffic is burstier than Poisson, which favours short timeouts after bursts end and predictive pre-warming for periodic patterns). Busy endpoints: short τ suffices. Rare endpoints: no τ is affordable—rely on fast cold starts, provisioned minimum replicas, or async APIs. Decide who pays for warm idle time.

Core How much extra capacity does regional redundancy cost, and how can you reduce it?

To lose one of r regions at peak without shedding: r/(r−1) × peak (2× for two regions, 1.5× for three, 1.33× for four). Reduce by: more regions; exploiting time-zone offsets (a failed region's peak coincides with others' off-peak); shedding flexible work first (batch, rollouts, free tiers); degrading (smaller model, shorter outputs); accepting residency-bound traffic cannot move; buying burst capacity on demand. State the business choice explicitly.

Deep-dive Walk through what happens when a zone fails in your LLM API design.

Detection: health checks and load-balancer probes fail within seconds; router ejects replicas. In-flight streams break; clients retry with idempotency keys under a retry budget. Capacity: region loses ~1/3; remaining zones run hotter—headroom plus autoscaler scale-up (limited by free GPUs in surviving zones); overflow non-residency traffic to other regions; pause batch; shed by tier if needed. State: weight caches in other zones, replicated control-plane store keeps quorum (three zones). Recovery: zone returns cold; warm caches, ramp gradually to avoid thundering herd. Afterwards: review whether zone spread and headroom matched the assumption.

Deep-dive How do large training gangs coexist with high utilisation and fast inference scale-up?

Reservations with backfill: compute the gang's earliest start, reserve nodes, fill them with preemptible batch/rollout work until then. Defragmentation keeps whole nodes available. Inference gets top priority, a headroom pool and a fast path; it can preempt batch instantly and, if necessary, training via checkpoint. Topology-aware placement gives gangs contiguous pods; inference spreads across racks. Flexible work is the buffer that makes both possible; without it you choose between idle reservations and starved gangs.

Design Design a retrieval service over one billion documents.

Estimate: 10⁹ × 1024 × 4 B ≈ 4 TB raw; PQ at 64 B/vector = 64 GB codes. Design: sharded IVF-PQ with in-memory codes and SSD re-ranking, or DiskANN-style graph on SSD; fan-out and merge; replicas per shard. Ingestion: batch embedding (GPU-hours estimate), streaming updates into a mutable delta index merged periodically. Query path: embed (small GPU/CPU), search, re-rank (optionally cross-encoder on top-k), filter by tenant/ACL inside the index. Tune n_probe/re-rank depth for recall ≥ 0.95 within 50 ms p99. Failures: shard replicas, partial-result signalling, index rebuilds. Cost: memory vs SSD vs recall.

Design Design a sandbox fleet for 50,000 concurrent coding-agent sessions.

Little's law: 50k live sandboxes; 2 vCPU/4 GiB each; busy ~20% → 4× CPU overcommit ≈ 25k vCPUs ≈ 130 servers; snapshot idle sandboxes. ~70 creations/s. MicroVMs from snapshots (sub-second), per-session workspace storage, exec API, network deny-by-default with allow-listed proxy, no credentials, resource limits, lifecycle and garbage collection. Placement by memory packing; durable session state so host loss costs one step. Observability and abuse detection. Coordinate with the LLM tier on KV policy during tool runs.

Core What are the most common mistakes in these interviews?

No numbers; sizing for the mean (ignoring peak-to-mean, headroom, redundancy); listing components without decisions; no control plane; no failure story; ignoring cold starts; treating GPUs like CPUs (scarce, slow to start, memory-bound, multi-GPU replicas); ignoring cost; not choosing among options; over-engineering before scoping; ignoring multi-tenancy and security; forgetting observability and rollout.

Deep-dive The interviewer says: "your model is replaced by one ten times larger." What changes?

Weights 10× (cold starts 10× longer; storage and distribution matter more); replicas span many more GPUs or multiple nodes (network becomes part of the replica; topology-aware placement, larger failure blast radius per replica); per-token cost up roughly 10× (fleet, power, budget; pricing and routing to smaller models for easy requests); KV per token larger (fewer concurrent sequences); fewer, larger replicas reduce routing flexibility and make each failure costlier (more headroom); autoscaling granularity coarser; rollout slower. Revisit capacity plan, redundancy and the tiering strategy.

Core What would you monitor on day one of operating an LLM serving fleet?

User-facing SLOs: TTFT and TPOT percentiles, error and refusal rates, goodput, by tier and region. Capacity: GPU allocation and utilisation, queue depth, KV memory, headroom vs forecast, warm-pool size, cold-start times. Efficiency: tokens per GPU-second, cache hit rate, cost per million tokens. Health: GPU errors (Xid, ECC), node failures, network errors. Control plane: rollout status, config versions, scheduler latency. Business: tokens by customer, rate-limit rejections. Alerts on SLO burn rates rather than raw thresholds.

Warm-up Give the two-minute summary of how to turn demand into a fleet and a budget.

Peak requests/s × output tokens ÷ (tokens/s per GPU × peak utilisation) = GPUs (per model tier; check prefill and KV memory). × 1.4 kW = IT power; × PUE = facility power. × $22k = annual hardware cost; add ~5% electricity, plus staff and network. Mean rate = peak ÷ peak-to-mean; annual tokens = mean rate × tokens × seconds per year; cost ÷ tokens = cost per million. Add redundancy factor r/(r−1), then recover utilisation with batch in the trough. Sanity-check against Atlas: 10k req/s mixed ≈ 4,000 H100s ≈ 5.6 MW ≈ $88M/yr.
