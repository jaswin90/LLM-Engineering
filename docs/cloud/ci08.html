Volume II · Part II · Orchestration — Chapter 8

Serving Platforms and Control Planes

An inference engine turns a prompt into tokens on one replica. A serving platform turns a model into a service: it deploys replicas from a registry, routes each request to a good replica, scales the fleet with demand, rolls out new versions safely, and exposes an endpoint with authentication, quotas and metrics. This chapter surveys the platforms that do this—from general model servers to LLM-native control planes and managed cloud endpoints—and develops the two quantitative problems at their core: choosing a replica for each request and choosing how many replicas to run.

Why this chapter

Chapters 6 and 7 gave us a cluster that can run containers on GPUs, place them sensibly and share capacity among workloads. Kubernetes knows about pods and GPUs, but nothing about models: it does not know that a replica has Llama 3 70B loaded with a particular LoRA adapter, that it holds a tenant's conversation prefix in its KV cache, that its decode batch is nearly full, or that the right signal for adding replicas is queued prefill tokens rather than CPU usage. Volume I described these concerns at the level of the fleet in Vol. I Chapter 27—routing with cache affinity, autoscaling with cold starts, multi-LoRA—without saying what software implements them. That software is the serving platform.

The landscape of serving platforms changed quickly between 2018 and 2026, and the reasons for each change are instructive. General-purpose model servers were built for small models with millisecond inference and request-level batching. LLMs broke their assumptions—token streaming, continuous batching inside the engine, KV caches that make replicas stateful, prefill and decode with different needs—and a new generation of LLM-native control planes appeared in 2024–2025. In parallel, the clouds turned model serving into managed products and per-token APIs, and a class of serverless GPU platforms emerged for spiky workloads. Interviewers expect a candidate to know what each layer does, to design the routing and autoscaling logic themselves, and to say when to build, when to adopt open source and when to buy.

We start with what a platform adds to an engine (§8.1), survey general model servers (§8.2) and the LLM-native control planes that followed (§8.3), and then develop replica selection (§8.4) and autoscaling (§8.5) quantitatively. We compare managed endpoints, token APIs and serverless GPUs (§8.6), describe the model lifecycle from registry to rollback (§8.7), and close with the build-versus-buy question (§8.8).

Learning objectives
  1. List the responsibilities a serving platform adds to an inference engine and draw the layers of a model-serving stack.
  2. Describe Triton, KServe, Ray Serve and BentoML, and explain why LLMs needed new control planes such as NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d and AIBrix.
  3. Explain the Gateway API Inference Extension and design a replica-scoring function that combines load, KV-cache pressure, prefix affinity and adapter locality.
  4. Choose autoscaling signals for LLM serving, apply the proportional scaling rule, and avoid overshoot and oscillation caused by cold-start delay.
  5. Compare managed endpoints, token APIs and serverless GPUs by cost, control and latency, including the break-even utilisation for serverless.
  6. Design a model lifecycle with registry, evaluation gates, shadow traffic, canaries and rollback.

Uses: Vol. I Chapters 11–13 and 27 (engines, disaggregation, fleet routing and autoscaling); Chapters 1, 5, 6 and 7 of this volume.

8.1 What a platform adds to an engine

An inference engine such as vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM (Vol. I Chapter 12) is a process that loads one model, accepts requests on an HTTP port, batches them continuously, and streams tokens back. Everything outside that process is the platform's job, and it helps to list the responsibilities explicitly, because each is a component that can be built, adopted or bought. Deployment: turn a declaration such as "Llama 3 70B, FP8, TP8, at least 40 replicas, in these zones" into running replicas with the right image, weights, arguments and resources. Endpoint management: give the model a stable name and URL, map model names and versions to replica pools, and support many models and adapters behind one API. Routing: choose a replica for each request, taking account of load and cache state. Autoscaling: decide how many replicas each pool needs, on the right signals. Lifecycle: register models, gate them on evaluation, roll new versions out gradually and roll back. Multi-tenancy: authenticate callers, enforce quotas and rate limits, meter usage for billing. Observability: collect per-request latency, tokens, errors and costs.

Figure 8.1 arranges these responsibilities into layers. The lowest platform layer runs and scales replicas (the Kubernetes machinery of Chapters 6–7 plus a controller that understands model deployments). Above it, a request-routing layer selects replicas. Above that, the API layer presents an OpenAI-compatible or provider-specific interface with authentication and limits (Chapter 11). Cutting across all of them are the lifecycle and observability systems. Different products cover different subsets: a general model server covers mostly the engine and part of deployment; an LLM control plane covers routing, autoscaling and disaggregation; a managed endpoint covers everything except the application.

8.2 General model servers: Triton, KServe, Ray Serve and BentoML

The first generation of serving platforms was designed before LLMs, for models such as image classifiers, recommenders and speech recognisers, and it established ideas that are still used. NVIDIA's Triton Inference Server, released in 2018 as the TensorRT Inference Server, introduced a model repository (a directory or bucket of models with configuration files), pluggable backends for TensorRT, PyTorch, ONNX Runtime, TensorFlow and Python, dynamic batching (collecting individual requests into batches up to a size or time limit), concurrent execution of several models on one GPU, and ensembles that chain models into pipelines (tokenizer → model → post-processing). A Triton backend for TensorRT-LLM later brought LLMs into the same server, with in-flight batching inside the engine.

KServe, begun in 2019 as KFServing within the Kubeflow project, is a Kubernetes-native platform built around a custom resource, the InferenceService, which declares a model's storage location, runtime and resources; KServe's controller turns it into deployments, services and routes. It standardised an inference protocol (the "v2" or Open Inference Protocol, shared with Triton), integrated Knative for request-driven autoscaling including scale to zero, offered canary rollouts by traffic splitting, and added ModelMesh for packing many small models densely onto shared servers. Later versions added LLM-specific runtimes and integration with the Gateway API Inference Extension discussed in §8.3.

Ray Serve, built on the Ray distributed computing framework, takes a programming-model view: a deployment is a Python class whose replicas Ray schedules across a cluster, deployments can call each other to form pipelines, and an autoscaler adjusts replica counts from queue lengths. It suits applications that compose several models with custom Python logic, and it is commonly used with vLLM for LLM serving and for batch inference (Chapter 19). BentoML packages models and serving code into deployable "bentos" with a focus on developer experience; its company also operates a managed platform. These systems remain useful, especially for non-LLM models and for pipelines around LLMs (embedding models, rerankers, classifiers, guardrails; Chapters 16 and 18).

The limitation of this generation for LLMs lies in assumptions buried in its design. It assumes requests are short and roughly uniform, so that request counts measure load and simple round-robin balances it; LLM requests vary by a thousand-fold in cost (Vol. I §27.1). It assumes replicas are stateless and interchangeable; LLM replicas carry prefix caches whose reuse can halve the cost of a request (Vol. I §27.2). It assumes batching happens in the server; for LLMs, batching happens inside the engine, step by step. And it has no notion of splitting a request's prefill and decode across machines (Vol. I Chapter 13). Each of these mismatches motivated the next generation.

8.3 LLM-native control planes

Between 2024 and 2025, several open-source control planes appeared that treat LLM serving as their primary case. Their designs differ, but they converge on the same set of capabilities: token- and cache-aware routing, autoscaling on LLM signals, disaggregated prefill and decode with KV-cache transfer, management of LoRA adapters, and tiered KV-cache storage beyond a single replica's HBM. The Gateway API Inference Extension deserves a closer look, because it turns replica selection into a standard component. Kubernetes' Gateway API is the successor to Ingress for configuring HTTP load balancers. The inference extension adds an InferencePool resource—a set of model-server pods sharing a base model—and model-level objectives such as criticality, and it defines an endpoint picker (EPP): an extension service that the gateway consults for each request. The gateway parses the request, tells the endpoint picker which model and adapter it wants, and the picker, which continuously scrapes each replica's metrics (queue length, KV-cache utilisation, loaded LoRA adapters) and may track which prefixes each replica holds, returns the replica to use. Figure 8.2 shows the flow. The design separates the generic parts of load balancing (connections, TLS, retries, which any gateway implements) from the LLM-specific choice of replica, which a pluggable picker implements.

Dynamo approaches the same problems from the engine side outward. Its router keeps a global index of which KV-cache blocks each worker holds, published by the workers as blocks are created and evicted, and routes each request to the worker that maximises cache reuse subject to load. Its planner watches prefill and decode queues and resizes the two pools of a disaggregated deployment, the dynamic version of the pool-sizing problem of Vol. I §13.5. Its KV block manager offloads cache blocks from HBM to host memory and storage and fetches them back on reuse, the hierarchical KV storage of Vol. I §10.7 made fleet-wide. Whatever the system, two decisions recur on every request and every few seconds: which replica, and how many replicas. We take them in turn.

One capability in Table 8.1 deserves its own discussion because it changes the economics of a platform: serving many models on one fleet. Enterprise platforms host hundreds or thousands of fine-tuned variants, most of them rarely used. Giving each its own replica pool wastes GPUs on idle replicas (Figure 3.4); packing them onto shared capacity requires the platform to move models and adapters in and out of GPU memory as demand shifts. For LoRA adapters (Vol. I §27.4), which are tens to hundreds of megabytes, the platform keeps a cache hierarchy analogous to the weight hierarchy of Chapter 5, shown in Figure 8.3: the adapters currently in use reside in each replica's HBM, a larger set in host memory, the whole catalogue on local NVMe or in object storage. A request for an adapter that is not in HBM incurs a load from the next tier—roughly 1 ms per 50 MB from host memory over PCIe, 10–50 ms from NVMe, a few hundred milliseconds from object storage—and the router prefers replicas that already hold it.

Figure 8.3 turns the platform's capacity problem into a caching problem with a simple cost model. If adapter requests follow a skewed popularity distribution—as they nearly always do, with a few heavy tenants and a long tail—then a replica whose HBM holds the top few dozen adapters serves most requests with no load at all, host memory catches most of the rest at a cost of milliseconds, and only the rare cold tenant pays a visible delay. Full fine-tunes are harder, since each is a whole model: the platform can keep popular ones resident, park others in sleep mode or host memory for second-scale swaps (§5.7), and scale rarely used ones to zero, accepting a cold start on first use. The platform's job is to choose these placements continuously from observed demand, and this is one of the features that distinguishes LLM platforms from general ones.

8.4 Choosing a replica: routing as scoring

Vol. I §27.2 analysed load balancing with queueing theory and showed that the power of two choices and join-the-shortest-queue cut tail latency dramatically compared with random assignment, and that cache affinity can save most of a request's prefill. A production picker must combine these objectives, which often conflict: the replica with the shortest queue rarely holds the request's prefix, and the replica that holds it may be busy. The standard approach is to filter and score, as cluster schedulers do (§7.1), but per request and in a millisecond.

Filtering removes replicas that cannot serve the request at all: they do not have the requested adapter and cannot load it quickly, they are not ready, their KV cache is too full to admit the request's context, or they are in the wrong region for the tenant's data (Chapter 12). Scoring estimates, for each remaining replica \(j\), the cost of serving the request there, and picks the cheapest. A useful form is an estimate of the request's time to first token:

\[ \widehat{\text{TTFT}}_j\approx\frac{n_{\text{q},j}}{\Phi_{P,j}}+\frac{n_{\text{in}}-n_{\text{hit},j}}{\Phi_{P,j}}+t_{\text{kv}}\,\mathbb 1[u^{\text{kv}}_j>u^{\text{kv}}_{\max}]+t_{\text{lora}}\,\mathbb 1[\text{adapter not loaded}], \]

where \(n_{\text{q},j}\) is the number of prefill tokens already queued at the replica, \(\Phi_{P,j}\) its prefill rate, \(n_{\text{hit},j}\) the length of the longest prefix of the request the replica already has cached, \(u^{\text{kv}}_j\) its KV-cache utilisation (above a threshold, admitting the request risks preemption of running sequences, Vol. I §11.5), and \(t_{\text{kv}},t_{\text{lora}}\) time penalties for those risks and for loading an adapter. The first term is queueing delay, the second the request's own uncached prefill, and the last two the cost of pushing a replica into trouble. Pickers in practice use simpler weighted sums of normalised metrics, but writing the score as an estimated latency makes the weights meaningful.

Worked example — which replica should take this request?

Request. A tenant's next chat turn: \(n_{\text{in}}=6{,}000\) tokens, of which the first 5,600 are the conversation so far; adapter a.

Replicas (as in Figure 8.2), each with \(\Phi_P=25{,}000\) prefill tokens/s. Replica 1: queue 4,000 tokens, KV 61%, holds the 5,600-token prefix, adapter a loaded. Replica 2: queue 0, KV 35%, no prefix, adapter not loaded (load cost 0.3 s). Replica 3: queue 30,000 tokens, KV 93% (above an 85% threshold, penalty 1 s), adapter a loaded, no prefix.

Scores. Replica 1: \(4000/25{,}000+400/25{,}000=0.16+0.016\approx0.18\) s. Replica 2: \(0+6000/25{,}000+0.3=0.54\) s. Replica 3: \(30{,}000/25{,}000+6000/25{,}000+1=2.44\) s.

Decision. Replica 1, despite having a longer queue than replica 2, because its cached prefix saves 5,600 tokens of prefill. A least-loaded router would have chosen replica 2 and spent three times the latency and fifteen times the prefill compute. A pure prefix-affinity router would also have chosen replica 1 here, but in a burst of turns from the same tenant it would pile them all onto replica 1 until its queue grew long; the score handles that trade-off automatically, because \(n_{\text{q},j}\) rises with each assignment.

Two refinements matter at scale. First, the picker's view of replica state is always slightly stale—metrics are scraped every tens of milliseconds, and many gateway instances route concurrently—so pure "pick the minimum score" can herd: every gateway sends its next requests to the same momentarily best replica. Sampling a few candidates and choosing the best among them (the power of \(n_c\) choices, Vol. I §27.2), or adding the picker's own recent assignments to each replica's queue estimate, prevents herding. Second, prefix ownership can be tracked cheaply without querying engines: if the router itself assigns requests by hashing the first block of the prompt (system prompt plus tenant) with bounded-load consistent hashing, it knows where each prefix lives, and engines' prefix caches naturally specialise. Both techniques appear in the control planes of Table 8.1.

Drill 8.1 — How much does prefix-aware routing save Atlas?

Atlas's 70B tier receives 3,500 requests/s at peak with 2,000 input tokens, of which 1,200 are a reusable prefix (system prompt and history). With 233 replicas and random routing, a request's prefix is cached on the chosen replica only if it was recently served there; suppose the per-replica hit rate is then 15%. With prefix-aware routing it is 60%. How much prefill compute per second does routing save, and how many replica-equivalents is that?

Solution. Random routing: uncached tokens per request \(2000-0.15\times1200=1820\); prefix-aware: \(2000-0.6\times1200=1280\). Saved: 540 tokens per request, \(3500\times540\approx1.9\times10^{6}\) prefill tokens/s. At 25,000 prefill tokens/s per replica, that is about 76 replica-equivalents of prefill capacity. Whether it saves 76 replicas depends on which phase binds (Chapter 1: decode binds for this tier at 60% hits); with random routing, prefill demand rises to \(3500\times1820/25{,}000\approx255\) replicas, overtaking decode's 233, so prefix-aware routing saves at least 22 replicas and keeps TTFT far lower.

8.5 How many replicas: autoscaling on the right signal

The autoscaler decides how many replicas each pool should run. Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA), and event-driven extensions such as KEDA, implement a simple rule: measure a metric \(m\) averaged over the current replicas, compare it with a target \(m^*\), and set

\[ n_{\text{desired}}=\Bigl\lceil n_{\text{current}}\cdot\frac{m}{m^*}\Bigr\rceil , \]

with a tolerance band to avoid reacting to noise and stabilisation windows that limit how fast replicas are removed. The rule assumes that the metric is proportional to load per replica, so that scaling the replica count by \(m/m^*\) brings the metric back to target. Everything depends on choosing a metric for which that assumption holds and which reflects the service's objectives.

For LLM serving, several obvious metrics are wrong. CPU utilisation, the HPA default, measures the engine's host process, which is busy whether the GPU is or not. GPU utilisation as reported by the driver is the fraction of time at least one kernel is running, not how much of the GPU is used, so a replica with a single sequence decoding can show near 100% while doing a few percent of its possible work; DCGM's SM-activity and tensor-pipe metrics are better but still do not reflect latency targets. Request rate is closer, but requests differ in cost by orders of magnitude. Better signals measure the engine's own load in its own units: the number of running and waiting sequences, queued prefill tokens, KV-cache utilisation (which approaches 100% just before the engine starts preempting), and, best of all, the quantity the service is judged on—SLO attainment, the fraction of recent requests meeting their TTFT and TPOT targets, or a high percentile of TTFT relative to its target. Production autoscalers combine a load signal for steady-state sizing with an SLO signal as a safety override.

For the mathematician — the autoscaler as a feedback controller with delay

The proportional rule is a discrete-time controller: at each step \(k\) it observes the load and sets a capacity target. If the observed load per replica is computed only over replicas that are serving, and new replicas take \(d\) steps to start (the cold start of Chapter 5), then during those \(d\) steps the controller sees the same overload and keeps adding replicas, as if the ones it already requested did not exist. The closed loop then overshoots by a factor of roughly \(d\) times the per-step correction. The fix is to count requested capacity (serving plus pending) when computing the correction, which Kubernetes' HPA does for pods but custom autoscalers built on engine metrics often do not. More generally, a feedback loop whose actuation delay is long compared with its sampling interval oscillates unless its gain is reduced in proportion; this is the classical stability problem of control with dead time, and it is why autoscalers for slow-starting replicas need either low gain, explicit accounting for pending capacity, or a predictive (feed-forward) component based on demand forecasts.

Figure 8.4 shows the effect in a simulation. Demand steps from a level needing 14 replicas to one needing 40; replicas take six steps (say, six minutes) to start. An autoscaler that counts pending replicas requests 26 more once and reaches 40. One that sizes from serving replicas only keeps requesting 26 more every step until the first new replicas arrive, ending with 170—more than four times the need—which a scale-down policy must then slowly undo.

Autoscaling LLM replicas has three further subtleties. Asymmetry: scaling up late breaks latency targets for everyone, while scaling down late only costs money, so policies scale up quickly and down slowly, with stabilisation windows of many minutes for scale-down. Prediction: because cold starts take tens of seconds to minutes, purely reactive scaling always lags a ramp; adding a forecast of demand from yesterday and last week (Chapter 9) lets the autoscaler start replicas before they are needed. Disaggregation: with separate prefill and decode pools, the autoscaler must size both, each on its own signal—prefill on queued prompt tokens and TTFT, decode on running sequences, KV utilisation and TPOT—and keep their ratio near the optimum of Vol. I §13.5 as the mix of prompt and output lengths changes. Dynamo's planner and llm-d's autoscaling components implement this two-pool control.

Worked example — choosing autoscaling targets for Atlas's 8B tier

Capacity per replica. At the 25 ms TPOT target, a replica sustains about 128 concurrent decoding sequences and 15,000 prefill tokens/s (Chapter 1).

Load signal. Use running plus waiting sequences per replica, target 96 (75% of 128). With 30,000 concurrent sequences fleet-wide, \(n_{\text{desired}}=\lceil30{,}000/96\rceil=313\).

Prefill guard. Also compute desired replicas from queued plus arriving prefill tokens at 75% of 15,000 tokens/s, and take the larger of the two answers—prefill binds this tier at peak (Chapter 1).

SLO override. If the 90th-percentile TTFT over the last minute exceeds 80% of its target, add 10% capacity immediately regardless of the load signals.

Dynamics. Count pending replicas; scale up with no stabilisation and scale down with a 15-minute window; add a forecast term that pre-provisions for the next 10 minutes of the daily ramp; keep a floor of replicas for failover (Chapter 13).

8.6 Managed endpoints, token APIs and serverless GPUs

Teams that do not want to operate any of the layers above can buy them. The options form a spectrum, matching Figure 1.3. Managed model endpoints on the hyperscalers—Amazon SageMaker endpoints, Google Vertex AI endpoints, Azure Machine Learning and AI Foundry managed online endpoints—deploy a customer's model container onto provider-managed instances with autoscaling, traffic splitting and monitoring; the customer chooses instance types and pays for instance-hours. Model-as-a-service APIs—Amazon Bedrock, Vertex AI's model garden, Azure's model catalogue, and the APIs of model labs and inference platforms (Together, Fireworks and others)—expose models by name and charge per million input and output tokens, with options to buy provisioned throughput, a reserved rate of tokens per minute for predictable latency. Serverless GPU platforms—Modal, Replicate, RunPod, Baseten and similar—run a customer's container on demand, bill by the second of GPU time, scale to zero when idle, and use the snapshotting and caching techniques of Chapter 5 to start containers in seconds.

The choice among them is mostly a function of utilisation and control. A dedicated replica costs its hourly price whether busy or idle. A serverless replica costs a higher price per second, but only while it is busy (plus cold-start time), so it wins when the workload is idle most of the time. If the dedicated price is \(c_{\text{ded}}\) per GPU-hour, the serverless price is \(c_{\text{sls}}>c_{\text{ded}}\) per busy GPU-hour, and the replica is busy a fraction \(u\) of the time with a cold-start overhead fraction \(o\) of busy time, serverless is cheaper when \(c_{\text{sls}}(1+o)u

Token APIs move the comparison from GPU-hours to tokens. A model-as-a-service price per million tokens includes the provider's utilisation, margin and engine optimisations. Vol. I §27.7 derived when self-hosting beats an API: only at high, steady utilisation and with an engine as efficient as the provider's. In practice organisations mix: token APIs for frontier proprietary models and for new or low-volume use cases, managed endpoints or serverless for moderate-volume custom models, and self-hosted platforms for the highest-volume models where the savings justify a team. Data governance can override cost: a regulated customer may require that prompts never leave its own cloud account, which rules out shared multi-tenant APIs unless they offer private deployment (Chapter 14).

Drill 8.2 — Serverless or dedicated for an internal tool?

An internal code-review assistant runs a fine-tuned 8B model. It receives bursts of requests during working hours, keeping a single H100 busy about 20% of those ten hours on weekdays and idle otherwise. Using Figure 8.5's prices, compare the monthly cost of one dedicated H100 with serverless, assuming cold starts add 10% to busy time.

Solution. Busy time per month: about 22 working days × 10 h × 0.2 = 44 GPU-hours, so \(u\approx44/730\approx6\%\). Dedicated: $1,825 a month. Serverless: \(44\times1.1\times\$4.50\approx\$218\) a month, about eight times cheaper—provided that the users tolerate the occasional cold start of a few seconds, or a warm instance is kept during working hours (\(220\) h × $4.50 = $990, still cheaper than dedicated).

8.7 The model lifecycle: from registry to rollback

Models change more often than engines. A provider ships new base versions every few months, fine-tunes weekly, adapters daily, and system-prompt or sampling-parameter changes continuously. Each change can improve quality or silently break it, and the serving platform must make changes safe. Figure 8.6 shows a lifecycle that mature platforms follow.

Each stage of Figure 8.6 guards against a different failure. Registration records the model's immutable digest, serving configuration (precision, parallelism, tokenizer, chat template, context limit) and provenance, so that every later stage tests exactly the bytes that will be served (§5.6). Offline gates run evaluation suites for quality and safety (Vol. I Chapter 28) and performance benchmarks for throughput and latency at the target configuration, catching both "the model got worse" and "the model got slower". Shadow traffic mirrors a sample of live requests to the new version and records its responses without returning them, revealing behaviour on the real prompt distribution—including the pathological prompts no evaluation suite contains—at the cost of the extra GPU time. Canaries route a small fraction of real users to the new version and compare its metrics with the old: latency, error rates, response lengths, refusal rates, user feedback signals and cost per token. Progressive rollout expands by failure domain and by tenant tier, so that a problem that escaped the canary is still contained. Throughout, the old version remains cached on nodes, so rollback is a fast change of digest rather than a slow re-deployment.

Two features distinguish model rollouts from code rollouts. Model regressions are often statistical—slightly more refusals, slightly longer answers, a drop in quality on one language—so canary analysis needs enough traffic and time for statistical tests to detect the effect sizes that matter, and the comparison should be between concurrent cohorts rather than before and after. And model changes can change cost as well as quality: a version that answers in 20% more tokens raises the fleet's decode load by 20% (Drill 1.1), so cost per request is a rollout metric alongside quality.

8.8 Build, adopt or buy

Every layer of Figure 8.1 can be built in-house, adopted from open source, or bought as a managed service, and the right answer differs by layer and by organisation. Engines are almost always adopted (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM) and modified only at the margins, because their pace of improvement is set by large communities and vendors. Routing and autoscaling were mostly built in-house until 2025, because nothing off the shelf understood LLMs; the arrival of llm-d, Dynamo, AIBrix and the Gateway API Inference Extension made adoption realistic, and many organisations now adopt one and extend its scoring and scaling policies. API gateways, rate limiting and metering are often bought or adopted from general web infrastructure, with LLM-specific token accounting added (Chapter 11). The lifecycle tooling—registry, evaluation pipelines, rollout analysis—is usually built, because it encodes the organisation's own quality bar.

Three questions decide each case. How differentiating is the layer: does doing it better than the default give a measurable advantage in cost, latency or quality? (For a model lab serving billions of tokens, a 10% better router is worth millions; for an enterprise serving one internal tool, it is not.) How fast is the layer changing: building on a moving target is expensive, but so is depending on a project that may not fit next year's architecture. And who will operate it at 3 a.m.? A managed service hands that question to the provider, at a price. The chapters of Part III quantify the price side of these decisions: what capacity costs, how to buy it, and how to account for it.

Looking ahead

Part II has taken us from containers to Kubernetes, from scheduling to serving platforms. We can now deploy models, route requests to good replicas and scale the fleet with demand. But the autoscaler can only add replicas if there are GPUs to put them on, and in the cloud GPUs are bought under very different terms—reserved for years, rented on demand, booked in blocks or taken as spot capacity that can vanish with two minutes' notice—at prices that differ by a factor of three or more. How much capacity should be bought on which terms, how should demand be forecast, and how should a fleet use spot capacity without hurting users? Chapter 9 opens Part III with these questions.

Chapter summary

  • A serving platform adds deployment, endpoint management, routing, autoscaling, lifecycle, multi-tenancy and observability to an engine; products cover different layers.
  • General model servers (Triton, KServe, Ray Serve, BentoML) introduced repositories, backends, dynamic batching, InferenceService CRDs, scale to zero and composition, but assumed short, uniform, stateless requests.
  • LLM-native control planes (NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d, AIBrix, vLLM production stack) add token- and cache-aware routing, LLM autoscaling, disaggregation with KV transfer, LoRA management and tiered KV storage; the Gateway API Inference Extension standardises replica selection through an endpoint picker.
  • Replica selection is filter-and-score per request; scoring by estimated TTFT combines queued prefill, uncached prompt length, KV pressure and adapter loading; sampling a few candidates and tracking own assignments prevents herding.
  • Autoscale on engine-native and SLO signals (running/waiting sequences, queued prefill tokens, KV utilisation, TTFT percentiles), not CPU or driver "GPU utilisation"; count pending capacity to avoid overshoot; scale up fast, down slowly; add forecasts; size prefill and decode pools separately.
  • Managed endpoints, token APIs, provisioned throughput and serverless GPUs trade control for convenience; serverless wins below a break-even utilisation \(u^*=c_{\text{ded}}/(c_{\text{sls}}(1+o))\).
  • Model lifecycle: immutable registration, offline gates, shadow traffic, statistical canaries, progressive rollout by failure domain, cost per request as a gate, rollback by digest.
  • Adopt engines; adopt-and-extend LLM control planes; build what encodes your differentiation and quality bar.

Warm-up What does a serving platform add to vLLM?

vLLM serves one model on one replica: batching, KV cache, kernels, an HTTP API. A platform adds: declarative deployment of replica pools from a registry; endpoint and model-name management for many models and adapters; per-request replica selection (load, cache, adapter aware); autoscaling on token and SLO signals with warm pools; disaggregation and KV transfer across replicas; lifecycle (evaluation gates, shadow, canary, progressive rollout, rollback); multi-tenancy (auth, quotas, rate limits, metering); and observability (latency, tokens, cost, errors per tenant and model).

Warm-up Why did LLM-specific control planes such as llm-d and Dynamo appear?

General model servers assumed short, uniform, stateless requests batched by the server and balanced by request counts. LLM requests vary by orders of magnitude in cost, stream tokens for seconds to minutes, are batched continuously inside the engine, and leave reusable KV state on replicas; prefill and decode have different resource needs and can be split across machines; many adapters share a base model. New control planes add token- and cache-aware routing, autoscaling on LLM signals, disaggregation with KV transfer (NIXL), LoRA management and tiered KV caches, plus a standard routing hook (Gateway API Inference Extension).

Warm-up Which metrics would you autoscale an LLM pool on, and which would you avoid?

Use: running plus waiting sequences per replica (decode load), queued prefill tokens (prefill load), KV-cache utilisation (headroom before preemption), and SLO attainment or TTFT/TPOT percentiles as an override; for disaggregated pools, separate signals per pool. Avoid: host CPU (busy regardless), driver GPU utilisation (time any kernel is active, saturates at low load), and raw request rate (costs vary). Count pending replicas, scale up fast and down slowly, and add forecasts for predictable ramps.

Core Design the replica-selection logic for a multi-tenant LLM pool with LoRA adapters and prefix caching.

Filter: ready replicas in allowed regions, KV capacity for the request's context, adapter loaded or loadable within a budget. Score each candidate by estimated TTFT: queued prefill tokens divided by prefill rate, plus uncached prompt tokens (request length minus the replica's cached prefix length) divided by prefill rate, plus penalties for high KV utilisation (preemption risk) and adapter loading. Choose the minimum among a sample of a few candidates (power of \(n_c\) choices) to avoid herding, and add own recent assignments to queue estimates. Track prefix ownership via bounded-load consistent hashing on the prompt's leading block, or via engine-published block indexes. Fall back gracefully when metrics are stale. Measure with A/B tests on TTFT and prefill compute.

Core Your autoscaler overshoots massively during ramps. Diagnose.

Likely the controller computes desired replicas from load observed on serving replicas and ignores pending ones: during the cold start it sees the same overload each interval and repeats the correction, overshooting by roughly cold-start time divided by the evaluation interval. Other causes: metrics lag, too-high gain, no stabilisation, signals that saturate (GPU utilisation). Fix: count requested (serving plus pending) capacity; lower gain or add damping; use stabilisation windows; add a forecast term for predictable ramps; shorten cold starts (Chapter 5); simulate the closed loop with realistic delays before deploying policy changes.

Core When would you choose a serverless GPU platform over dedicated replicas? Quantify.

Serverless bills only busy time (plus cold starts) at a premium; dedicated bills every hour. Break-even busy fraction \(u^*=c_{\text{ded}}/(c_{\text{sls}}(1+o))\); with $2.50/h dedicated and $4.50/busy-hour serverless with 5% overhead, about 53%. Spiky, mostly idle workloads (internal tools, prototypes, many low-traffic fine-tunes) favour serverless—an internal tool busy 6% of the month costs about $220 versus $1,825. Steady high-volume traffic favours dedicated or reserved capacity. Also weigh cold-start latency tolerance, data governance, GPU availability on the platform, and lock-in. Combine: dedicated base, serverless burst.

Core Describe a safe rollout process for a new model version.

Register immutable digest plus serving config and provenance. Offline gates: quality and safety evals, performance benchmarks at target config (throughput, TTFT/TPOT, cost per request). Shadow: mirror a sample of live traffic, compare outputs and lengths, look for pathological prompts. Canary: 1–5% of traffic with concurrent control cohort; compare latency, errors, response length, refusal rate, user signals, cost; statistical tests sized to detect relevant effects. Progressive rollout by failure domain and tenant tier with automated pause and rollback triggers. Keep old version cached; rollback by digest. Post-rollout monitoring for slow-emerging issues.

Core Explain the Gateway API Inference Extension and why it is a useful abstraction.

It extends Kubernetes' Gateway API with an InferencePool (a set of model-server pods for a base model) and model-level objectives (e.g. criticality), and defines an endpoint-picker protocol: for each request, the gateway asks an extension service which pod to use. The picker scrapes engine metrics (queues, KV utilisation, loaded adapters) and may track prefixes, implementing LLM-aware selection. Benefit: separation of concerns—any conforming gateway (Envoy-based and others) handles TLS, auth, retries and streaming, while routing intelligence is a pluggable, swappable component shared across projects like llm-d and KServe.

Deep-dive Analyse the stability of a proportional autoscaler with cold-start delay.

Model: at interval \(k\), demand \(D_k\); serving capacity \(S_k\); the controller orders \(a_k=\max(0,D_k/u^*-C_k)\) where \(C_k\) is the capacity it believes it has; orders arrive after \(d\) intervals. If \(C_k=S_k\) (serving only), after a step in demand the same deficit is ordered for \(d\) consecutive intervals, giving roughly \(d\)-fold overshoot; with scale-down also proportional and delayed, the system oscillates. If \(C_k=S_k+\) pending, the deficit is ordered once: deadbeat response. With noisy metrics, a gain \(<1\) plus stabilisation reduces chatter at the cost of slower response; dead time limits achievable bandwidth (a controller cannot usefully react faster than the delay). Feed-forward from forecasts handles predictable ramps without high feedback gain.

Deep-dive How do prefix-aware routing and load balancing conflict, and how do you resolve it?

Affinity sends related requests (same tenant/system prompt/conversation) to the replica holding their prefix, saving prefill; but popular prefixes then overload a few replicas, raising queueing delay and tail latency, and hurting fairness. Pure load balancing spreads load but discards cache reuse. Resolve with a cost model in common units (estimated TTFT or GPU-seconds): queueing delay plus uncached prefill plus penalties, so the router accepts a longer queue only when the prefix saving exceeds it; bounded-load consistent hashing caps any replica's share (e.g. 1.25× average) and spills to the next; replicate hot prefixes to several replicas; sample candidates to avoid herding; and measure both TTFT and prefill compute.

Deep-dive How would you autoscale a disaggregated prefill/decode deployment?

Two pools with separate signals: prefill sized on queued and arriving uncached prompt tokens per second relative to per-replica prefill rate, guarded by TTFT percentiles; decode sized on running sequences and KV utilisation relative to targets, guarded by TPOT percentiles. Keep the pool ratio near the optimum implied by the traffic mix (prompt/output lengths, cache hit rate), recomputing as the mix changes (a planner, as in Dynamo). Account for KV-transfer bandwidth and its coupling (prefill bursts create decode admission bursts). Count pending capacity in each pool, scale up fast and down slowly, and allow role switching if engines can change roles faster than cold starts.

Design Design a serving platform for 300 enterprise customers, each with a fine-tuned 8B or 70B model.

Classify customers: LoRA fine-tunes (most) served via multi-LoRA base pools (8B and 70B) with adapter-aware routing and an adapter cache hierarchy (HBM, host, NVMe, object store); full fine-tunes on dedicated pools; isolation-requiring customers on MIG slices or dedicated replicas. Control plane: model registry with per-customer digests and configs; Gateway with auth, per-customer rate limits and metering; endpoint picker scoring by load, KV, adapter presence and prefix; autoscaling per base pool on sequences/prefill/SLO, with scale-to-zero (and fast reload) for idle full fine-tunes; serverless-style warm pool. Lifecycle per customer with eval gates and canaries. Observability per tenant (cost per token for chargeback). Capacity: reserved base, on-demand burst.

Design Would you build or adopt your LLM serving control plane? Argue it.

Depends on scale, differentiation and team. Adopt engines (vLLM/SGLang/TensorRT-LLM). For routing and autoscaling, adopt an open control plane (llm-d, Dynamo, AIBrix, KServe with the inference extension) to get standard capabilities quickly, then extend scoring and scaling policies where measurements show gains—worth it at billions of tokens per day where 10% efficiency is millions per year. Build what encodes the organisation's own quality bar and business: registry policies, evaluation gates, rollout analysis, tenant pricing. Buy gateways, rate limiting and observability infrastructure where good products exist. Revisit as the ecosystem matures; avoid forks that cannot follow upstream.

Core Compare managed endpoints, model-as-a-service APIs and self-hosting for a 70B open model.

Managed endpoint (SageMaker, Vertex, Azure): your container on provider-managed instances; pay instance-hours; provider handles scaling, rollout, monitoring; you choose engine and hardware; costs like self-hosting plus a margin, less ops. Model-as-a-service (Bedrock, Vertex model garden, Together, Fireworks): pay per token or provisioned throughput; no infrastructure; provider's optimisations and utilisation included; less control over versions, latency and data handling. Self-hosting on Kubernetes: lowest unit cost at high, steady utilisation with a strong team; highest operational burden. Decide on volume and utilisation, latency and governance needs, and team capacity; many organisations start with MaaS and move high-volume, steady models to self-hosting.

Deep-dive What makes canary analysis for LLM model versions hard, and how would you design it?

Regressions are statistical and multi-dimensional (quality, refusal rate, response length, latency, cost, safety), often small, and outputs are stochastic; traffic mix varies over the day, confounding before/after comparisons; quality signals are delayed or sparse (user feedback). Design: concurrent randomised cohorts (by user or session, sticky), pre-registered metrics with minimum detectable effects and sample-size calculations, sequential testing with error control, LLM-judge or reference-based quality sampling on canary vs control, guardrail metrics (safety classifier rates), latency and cost per request, automated pause thresholds, and stratification by tenant and task type. Run long enough to cover diurnal patterns where needed.

Core How does a router avoid sending requests to replicas with stale metrics?

Metrics are scraped every tens of milliseconds and many gateway instances route concurrently, so state is always stale. Techniques: add the router's own in-flight assignments to each replica's queue estimate until the next scrape reflects them; use power-of-\(n_c\)-choices sampling rather than global minimum; apply short-lived penalties after assigning to a replica; prefer engines that push load updates or piggyback load on responses; cap per-replica share (bounded load); and detect unhealthy replicas by response errors and latency, not only metrics. These keep the system from herding and from oscillating between replicas.
