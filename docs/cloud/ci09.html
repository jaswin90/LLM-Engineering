Capacity, Procurement and Autoscaling

The autoscaler of Chapter 8 can add a replica only if a GPU is available to run it, and in the cloud GPUs are bought on very different terms: by the hour with no guarantee, by the year at a discount, in blocks booked weeks ahead, or as spare capacity that the provider can reclaim with two minutes' notice. This chapter treats capacity as an economic and probabilistic problem: which terms exist, how much capacity to commit to, how to forecast demand, how to use preemptible capacity safely, how to size warm pools, and what to do when there is simply not enough.

Why this chapter

In classical cloud computing, capacity was an afterthought: a team asked for another virtual machine and got it within a minute, at a published price. GPU capacity in 2023–2026 did not work that way. The most useful accelerators were sold out for long periods, clouds rationed them through quotas, and the price of the same H100-hour varied by a factor of three or four depending on whether it was rented on demand, reserved for three years, booked as a block or taken as spot. For a serving organisation, the choice of purchasing terms moves the cost of every token as much as all of Volume I's engine optimisations combined, and the risk of being short of capacity at the daily peak is the risk of an outage.

The questions are quantitative, and interviewers ask them that way: "our demand peaks at 2,500 GPUs and troughs at 1,150; how many should we reserve?", "can we serve production traffic on spot GPUs?", "how big should the warm pool be?", "we have a launch in six weeks; how do we make sure we have capacity?". Each has an answer derived from a demand model and a price list, and each answer depends on assumptions—about forecast error, about the availability of on-demand capacity, about what idle capacity can be used for—that must be stated.

We begin with why capacity binds (§9.1) and the menu of purchase options (§9.2). We derive the optimal amount of committed capacity, which turns out to be a newsvendor problem (§9.3), and discuss how to forecast the demand it depends on (§9.4). We analyse spot capacity and preemption (§9.5), fleet-level autoscaling with warm pools (§9.6), what to do when capacity runs out (§9.7), and the planning process that ties it together (§9.8).

Learning objectives
  1. Explain why GPU capacity, unlike ordinary cloud capacity, must be planned and procured in advance.

  5. Compute the slowdown of preemptible jobs with and without checkpoints, and decide when spot capacity can carry serving traffic.

9.1 Capacity as the binding constraint

Ordinary cloud capacity behaves like a utility: there is so much of it, spread over so many customers, that any one customer's demand is a rounding error, and the provider can promise to supply whatever is asked for at a fixed price. This works because of statistical multiplexing—millions of customers' demands are nearly independent, so their total is smooth and predictable—and because CPU servers are cheap enough for providers to keep large buffers. GPU capacity in the 2020s broke both conditions. Demand was dominated by a few very large customers whose needs were correlated (everyone wanted the newest GPU at the same time), and the hardware was so expensive and supply-constrained that no provider could afford large idle buffers.

The consequences shape every capacity decision. First, lead time: a large block of new GPUs takes months to obtain, whether by contract with a cloud or neocloud or by building (Chapter 2). Capacity for next quarter's peak must be committed this quarter. Second, asymmetric risk: being long (holding capacity that is not used) costs money, while being short (lacking capacity at the peak) causes latency violations, rejected requests and lost revenue, and possibly a public outage. Third, quotas and availability: on-demand GPU capacity may be listed but unobtainable, especially for large instances at peak hours in popular regions, so "we will just scale up on demand" is a plan that can fail exactly when it is needed. A capacity plan must therefore decide in advance how much capacity to secure on which terms, and what to do when demand exceeds it.

9.2 The menu: how GPU capacity is sold

Clouds and neoclouds sell accelerator capacity on five broad kinds of terms, arranged in Figure 9.1 from most flexible to most committed. The exact names, discounts and rules differ between providers and change often; the structure is stable.

On-demand capacity is rented by the hour or second with no commitment, at the list price. It is the most flexible and the most expensive, and for scarce GPUs its availability is not guaranteed: a request may fail for lack of capacity. AWS's on-demand price for an eight-H100 instance was about $98 per hour (about $12 per GPU-hour) at launch in 2023, and was cut by roughly 45% in mid-2025, illustrating both the premium and how fast prices move. Reserved instances and committed-use discounts trade a one- or three-year commitment, paid whether used or not, for discounts that for GPUs often reach 40–60% of on-demand, and—critically—for an assurance that the capacity exists. Savings plans are a variant that commits to a dollar amount of spending rather than to specific instances.

Capacity blocks are a newer product designed for GPUs: the customer books a fixed number of instances for a fixed period of days or weeks, starting at a future date, at a price set at booking. AWS introduced EC2 Capacity Blocks for ML in 2023, and Google's Dynamic Workload Scheduler offers comparable calendar-mode reservations and a "flex-start" mode that waits for capacity to become available. Blocks fit training runs, evaluation campaigns and launches. Spot (AWS, Azure) or preemptible and Spot VMs (Google) sell spare capacity at a large discount—up to 60–90% off on-demand—with the condition that the provider can reclaim it, after a short notice (two minutes on AWS, about 30 seconds on Google Cloud). For GPUs, spot capacity is often scarce precisely because the GPUs are fully used, so its availability fluctuates. Finally, long-term contracts with clouds and neoclouds—multi-year, take-or-pay agreements for dedicated clusters, often negotiated before the hardware is even installed—account for much of the frontier labs' capacity and offer the lowest effective price per GPU-hour at the cost of the largest commitment.

The menu presents a portfolio problem. Committed capacity is cheap per hour but expensive when idle; on-demand capacity is expensive per hour and may not be there; spot is cheapest but unreliable. How much of each should a serving fleet hold?

9.3 How much to commit: a newsvendor problem

Consider first a simplified version with two options. Demand for GPUs over a period of length \(T_{\text{per}}\) (a day, say, repeated for the life of the commitment) is a function \(D(\tau)\). We commit to \(R\) GPUs at price \(c_r\) per GPU-hour, paid for all of \(T_{\text{per}}\), and buy any excess, \(\max(D(\tau)-R,0)\), on demand at \(c_o>c_r\). The cost per period is

\[ C(R)=c_rT_{\text{per}}R+c_o\int_0^{T_{\text{per}}}\max\bigl(D(\tau)-R,0\bigr)\,d\tau . \]
Proposition 9.1 — The critical ratio for committed capacity

If on-demand capacity is always available, \(C(R)\) is convex and is minimised at the level \(R^*\) for which the fraction of time that demand exceeds it equals the price ratio:

\[ \frac{1}{T_{\text{per}}}\bigl|\{\tau:D(\tau)>R^*\}\bigr|=\frac{c_r}{c_o}. \]

Proof. The integrand \(\max(D-R,0)\) is convex in \(R\), so \(C\) is convex. Its derivative is \(C'(R)=c_rT_{\text{per}}-c_o\,|\{\tau:D(\tau)>R\}|\), since increasing \(R\) by \(dR\) saves \(c_o\,dR\) for every instant at which demand exceeds \(R\). Setting \(C'(R^*)=0\) gives the condition. \(\square\)

Proposition 9.1 is the newsvendor result of operations research in a new guise: a newspaper seller who must order papers before knowing demand should order up to the quantile of demand at which the probability of selling the last paper equals the ratio of its cost to its price. Here the "probability" is the fraction of time, and the rule reads: commit to the level of demand that is exceeded a fraction \(c_r/c_o\) of the time. If committed capacity costs 40% of on-demand, commit to the level exceeded 40% of the day; if 25%, to the level exceeded a quarter of the day. Figure 9.2 shows the cost for Atlas's 70B tier, whose demand we model as \(D(\tau)=1825+675\cos(2\pi(\tau-13)/24)\) GPUs (peak 2,500 at 13:00, trough 1,150), with \(c_r=\$2.50\) and \(c_o=\$6.90\).

Figure 9.2 carries three lessons. First, committing is worth a great deal: going from all on-demand to the optimum halves the cost. Second, the optimum is closer to the peak than to the mean, because on-demand capacity is so expensive: here about 2,100 GPUs, with on-demand covering the top five or six hours of each day. Third, the curve is flat near the optimum—committing to the full peak costs only about 6% more—so errors in the forecast matter less than they might seem, and the flatness is the right place to spend robustness.

The simplified model ignores three features of GPU capacity, and each pushes the commitment upward. On-demand may be unavailable: if there is some probability that peak capacity cannot be bought, each GPU-hour of shortfall carries an additional penalty \(c_{\text{short}}\)—lost revenue, latency violations—and the critical ratio becomes \(c_r/(c_o+p_{\text{na}}c_{\text{short}})\) with \(p_{\text{na}}\) the probability of non-availability, which for scarce GPUs can push \(R^*\) to the peak or above it. Idle committed capacity has value: if troughs can be lent to batch inference, training or rollouts (§7.7), an idle committed GPU-hour is worth \(c_{\text{idle}}\) rather than nothing, so the effective cost of committing falls to \(c_r-c_{\text{idle}}\) during troughs, again pushing \(R^*\) up. Demand grows and is uncertain: a three-year commitment made against a forecast that may be wrong in either direction is a bet, and the condition of Proposition 9.1 must be applied to a distribution of future demand curves, not one. These considerations explain why serving organisations in 2023–2026 typically committed to most or all of their expected peak, kept on-demand and spot for bursts and batch, and used the troughs for other work.

Figure 9.3 shows what the resulting portfolio looks like over one day. The committed base, here drawn at the peak-hugging level the scarcity analysis below recommends, covers nearly all of inference demand; the band between inference's curve and the commitment, wide at night, is lent to preemptible work; on-demand capacity covers any excursion above the commitment; and spot capacity runs batch work outside the commitment altogether, wherever it is cheap and available.

Worked example — committing when on-demand is unreliable

Setup. As in Figure 9.2, but on-demand H100 capacity at the peak is unavailable with probability \(p_{\text{na}}=0.3\), and a GPU-hour of unserved peak demand costs \(c_{\text{short}}=\$20\) in lost revenue and SLO penalties. Troughs can be lent to batch work worth \(c_{\text{idle}}=\$1.25\) per GPU-hour.

Critical ratio. The marginal committed GPU-hour costs \(c_r=2.50\) when demand exceeds it and \(c_r-c_{\text{idle}}=1.25\) when it does not; the marginal on-demand alternative costs \(c_o+p_{\text{na}}c_{\text{short}}=6.90+6=12.90\). Balancing, the optimal \(R\) satisfies \(f\,(12.90-2.50)=(1-f)\times1.25\), where \(f\) is the fraction of time demand exceeds \(R\): \(f\approx0.107\).

Commitment. Demand exceeds \(R\) for 10.7% of the day when \(\cos\) exceeds \(\cos(0.107\pi)\approx0.944\), i.e. \(R\approx1825+0.944\times675\approx2{,}462\): within 2% of the peak.

Lesson. Once scarcity and the value of troughs are included, the rational commitment for a latency-critical tier is essentially the peak. The cost of holding the trough is then recovered by lending it, which is why capacity planning and the cluster sharing of Chapter 7 are one problem.

9.4 Forecasting demand

Every capacity decision rests on a forecast, and the forecast must answer two questions at different horizons. For procurement, months ahead: what will the peak be next quarter and next year, and how uncertain is it? For autoscaling, minutes to hours ahead: how much will demand rise over the next ten minutes, the cold-start horizon of Chapter 5? Both are time-series forecasts with the same ingredients, and LLM traffic has a recognisable structure. It has strong daily seasonality (the working day of the customers' time zones), weekly seasonality (weekdays above weekends), a trend (growth of the product, often tens of percent per quarter), and events—launches, marketing campaigns, a new model version, a viral application built on the API, an outage at a competitor—that produce step changes and spikes.

Simple methods go a long way. A seasonal naive forecast ("same time last week, scaled by recent growth") is hard to beat for short horizons; exponential smoothing with trend and seasonality (Holt–Winters) and decomposition models such as Prophet handle several seasonalities and holidays; gradient-boosted or neural models add covariates such as planned launches. More important than the method is the form of the output. A capacity plan needs not a single number but quantiles: the level that demand will exceed with probability 50%, 10%, 1%. Planning to the median forecast leaves the fleet short half the time; planning to the 99th percentile of a wide distribution wastes money. The right quantile follows from the same critical-ratio reasoning as Proposition 9.1: provision committed capacity to the quantile of forecast demand whose exceedance probability equals the ratio of the cost of excess to the cost of shortage.

For the mathematician — quantile forecasts and the pinball loss

A forecast of the \(q\)-quantile of a random demand \(D\) is the minimiser of the expected pinball loss \(\mathbb E[\ell_q(D-\hat d)]\), where \(\ell_q(x)=qx\) for \(x\ge0\) and \((q-1)x\) for \(x<0\). Differentiating, the optimum satisfies \(\Pr(D\le\hat d)=q\). The loss weighs under-forecasts by \(q\) and over-forecasts by \(1-q\), which is exactly the asymmetry of capacity: under-provisioning costs \(c_{\text{short}}\) per unit, over-provisioning costs \(c_{\text{over}}\), and the optimal provision is the quantile \(q=c_{\text{short}}/(c_{\text{short}}+c_{\text{over}})\). Training forecasting models with the pinball loss at the business-relevant quantile, rather than with squared error (which targets the mean), aligns the model with the decision. Evaluation should check calibration: demand should exceed the 90% forecast about 10% of the time.

For LLM services, one feature makes forecasting harder than for most web traffic: the unit of demand changes. A forecast of requests per second is not enough, because the GPUs needed per request depend on input and output lengths, cache-hit rates and the model mix, all of which drift: a new reasoning mode doubles output lengths (Drill 1.1), a prompt-template change destroys cache hits (Vol. I Drill 27.3), a customer migrates from the 8B to the 70B tier. Capacity forecasts should therefore be made in the units that map to GPUs—prefill and decode tokens per second per model, after cache hits—and product changes that alter those units should pass through capacity review like any other launch.

Drill 9.1 — Which quantile to plan for?

Next quarter's peak demand for Atlas's 70B tier is forecast with median 2,900 GPUs and a standard deviation of 300 (approximately normal). Committed capacity costs $2.50 per GPU-hour; a GPU-hour of shortfall at the peak costs about $13 (on-demand at $6.90 when available, lost revenue when not). At what quantile, and to what level, should committed capacity be set, ignoring the value of troughs?

Solution. Over-provisioning costs \(c_{\text{over}}=2.50\) per unused GPU-hour; under-provisioning costs \(c_{\text{short}}-c_r\approx10.5\) net per GPU-hour short (we would have paid \(c_r\) anyway). \(q=10.5/(10.5+2.5)\approx0.81\). The 81st percentile of \(N(2900,300^2)\) is \(2900+0.88\times300\approx3{,}160\) GPUs. Planning to the median would leave the tier short of capacity at the peak on almost half of the days.

9.5 Spot capacity and preemption

Spot capacity is the cheapest GPU time available, and for some workloads it is ideal. Its defining property is that it can be taken away: when the provider needs the capacity for on-demand or reserved customers, it sends a short notice and terminates the instance. Whether a workload can use spot depends on what an interruption costs it, and that can be computed.

Consider first a batch job that needs \(L\) hours of uninterrupted work and makes no checkpoints, on capacity that is reclaimed as a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda_p\) per hour. Each interruption loses all progress and forces a restart. The expected total time to completion is a classical result:

\[ \mathbb E[T_{\text{done}}]=\frac{e^{\lambda_pL}-1}{\lambda_p}, \]

so the slowdown factor \((e^{\lambda_pL}-1)/(\lambda_pL)\) grows exponentially with the expected number of interruptions during one attempt, \(\lambda_pL\). A job expected to see one interruption per attempt takes 1.7 times its ideal time; three interruptions, more than six times. With checkpoints every \(\tau_c\) hours (and negligible checkpoint cost), the job is a sequence of short jobs of length \(\tau_c\), each with slowdown \((e^{\lambda_p\tau_c}-1)/(\lambda_p\tau_c)\approx1+\lambda_p\tau_c/2\), so frequent checkpoints make the exponent disappear. Figure 9.4 compares the two.

For batch inference, the "checkpoint" is simply the set of completed items: a job that processes a billion prompts writes each result as it finishes, so an interruption loses only the items in flight, and spot capacity is nearly perfect for it (Chapter 19). For reinforcement-learning rollouts, which are also inference, the same holds. For training, asynchronous checkpoints make spot viable when interruptions are not too frequent (§7.6), though large synchronous jobs suffer because any one node's interruption stops all of them.

Online serving is the hard case. A serving replica on spot capacity can be reclaimed at any moment, taking its in-flight requests and its prefix cache with it, and its replacement needs a cold start. Spot can still carry serving traffic if three conditions hold. The fleet must tolerate the loss of a spot replica, which means the replicas are a minority of capacity or have spare capacity behind them. The two-minute notice must be enough to drain: stop admitting requests, let short requests finish, and hand long ones to other replicas by resumable streams (§6.7). And spot interruptions must not be strongly correlated: if the provider reclaims a whole pool of instances at once, as happens when a region's demand spikes, many spot replicas disappear together, and the fleet must survive that correlated event. The usual answer is diversification: spread spot capacity over several instance types, zones and even regions, whose reclamations are less correlated, and treat spot as an overlay on top of committed capacity that covers the SLO on its own.

Worked example — can Atlas-Small run partly on spot?

Setup. At peak, Atlas-Small needs 427 GPUs at its target utilisation (Chapter 1). Committed H100s cost $2.50 per GPU-hour, spot $1.00. Spot is reclaimed at an average rate of one interruption per instance per 20 hours, but in bad hours a pool can lose 50% of its spot instances at once.

Design. Hold committed capacity sufficient to meet the SLO at a degraded utilisation of 90% if all spot disappears: \(427\times0.75/0.9\approx356\) committed GPUs. Add 71 spot GPUs, spread over three instance types and two zones, to bring normal utilisation back to the 75% target.

Saving. \(71\times(2.50-1.00)\times24\approx\$2{,}560\) per day, about $0.9 million a year—about 17% of the tier's GPU cost—at the price of running hotter (worse tail latency) whenever spot is lost. With 1-GPU replicas, 30-second cold starts from local caches and resumable streams, each interruption costs a few seconds of extra latency for the affected requests.

When not to. For the 70B tier, each spot interruption removes an 8-GPU replica and its cache, and a correlated reclaim of 50% would remove dozens of replicas at once; the committed base must then be larger and the saving smaller. For DeepSeek-V3's multi-node replicas, a single instance's reclaim takes down a whole group, so spot is rarely worth it.

9.6 Fleet-level autoscaling and warm pools

Chapter 8 developed the replica autoscaler, which decides how many replicas each pool should run. Underneath it, the fleet must supply GPUs for those replicas, and at that level two time constants matter: the time to start a replica on an existing node (\(t_{\text{cold}}\) without \(t_{\text{node}}\), seconds to a minute when optimised) and the time to obtain a new node from the cloud (\(t_{\text{node}}\), minutes when available, never when not). A fleet whose replica autoscaler can act in thirty seconds but whose nodes take five minutes to appear behaves like a five-minute system during ramps, unless it holds a warm pool: nodes that are provisioned, booted, joined to the cluster, with images pulled and weights cached, but not running replicas.

The warm pool's size follows from the same reasoning as headroom (§5.8): it must cover the demand growth that can occur during one node-provisioning time. If demand can rise at \(\dot\lambda\) GPUs per minute, the pool needs at least \(w=\dot\lambda\,t_{\text{node}}\) GPUs to bridge the gap while new nodes are requested, plus enough to absorb the failure of the largest failure domain (Chapter 13). Its cost is \(w\,c_{\text{gpu}}\) per hour, unless—as in §7.7—the warm nodes run preemptible work that can be evicted in seconds, in which case they are "warm" in the sense that a replica can start there quickly, and their cost is largely recovered. In a fleet built on committed capacity, the "warm pool" is simply the portion of committed capacity lent to borrowers, and fleet autoscaling becomes reclamation.

Fleet-level autoscaling has a second job: moving capacity between models and tiers. Atlas's three tiers peak at slightly different times (enterprise traffic for the 70B tier peaks during business hours, consumer traffic for the 8B tier peaks in the evening), and a fleet that can reassign GPUs between tiers needs less total capacity than one with fixed partitions—the statistical multiplexing that ordinary clouds enjoy, regained within one organisation. The enablers are the same as before: fast cold starts, nodes that can host any tier's replicas (weights for all tiers cached locally), and a scheduler that treats tiers as priority classes over a shared pool rather than as separate clusters.

Drill 9.2 — Sizing a warm pool

During the morning ramp, Atlas's 70B tier grows by about 15 GPUs per minute (1.9 replicas of eight). New nodes take 6 minutes to provision when available. Replica cold starts on warm nodes take 35 s. How large a warm pool is needed to keep up with the ramp, and what does it cost per day if held for 4 hours and idle?

Solution. \(w=15\times6=90\) GPUs (about 12 nodes), plus the 35 s cold start's worth of growth (about 9 GPUs) as replica-level headroom: about 100 GPUs. Idle for 4 hours at $2.50 per GPU-hour: \(100\times4\times2.5=\$1{,}000\) per day. If instead those nodes run batch work that can be preempted in under a minute, the warm pool costs only the lost batch progress. If node provisioning can fail outright at peak (scarce on-demand), the warm pool must be committed capacity, and its size is set by the forecast quantile of §9.4 rather than by the ramp rate.

9.7 When capacity runs out

However carefully capacity is planned, there will be moments when demand exceeds it: a forecast miss, a viral launch, the loss of a zone, a provider that cannot deliver on-demand capacity. A well-designed service degrades gracefully instead of failing, and the options form a ladder from invisible to visible. First, use other capacity: spill traffic to other regions that have headroom, if latency and data-residency rules allow (Chapter 12), or to another cloud or neocloud where the service is deployed. Second, run hotter: raise target utilisation and batch sizes, accepting higher latency within the SLO's slack (Vol. I §11.8 shows how steeply queueing delay rises, so this buys only a little). Third, shed deferrable work: pause batch jobs, evaluations and rollouts that share the fleet, and reclaim their capacity. Fourth, degrade quality gracefully: route some traffic from a large model to a smaller one (a cascade, Vol. I §27.6), shorten maximum output lengths, disable expensive features such as long reasoning modes for free-tier users. Fifth, prioritise: admit paying and high-priority tenants and reject or queue others with clear errors and retry-after headers (Chapter 11). Each rung should be a pre-planned, tested switch, not an improvisation during an incident.

Figure 9.5 also shows why capacity planning cannot be separated from product decisions. Whether free-tier users may be moved to a smaller model under load, which tenants have contractual priority, and how long a queue is acceptable are product and commercial choices. The infrastructure's job is to make each rung available, measurable and fast to switch on.

9.8 The capacity-planning process

Large serving organisations run capacity planning as a recurring process, typically quarterly with monthly updates, and its structure is worth knowing because interviews for infrastructure leads ask about it. The process starts with a demand plan: forecasts of tokens per second by model, tier and region, with quantiles, combining statistical forecasts with known launches and customer commitments. It converts demand into a supply requirement using measured per-replica throughput for each hardware type and engine configuration (the Atlas calculation of Chapter 1, at the chosen forecast quantile), plus spare capacity for failures and rollouts (Chapter 13) and headroom for cold starts. It compares the requirement with current and contracted supply and decides what to procure on which terms, considering lead times: long-term contracts and reservations for the base, capacity blocks for planned events, on-demand and spot for bursts. And it feeds back efficiency targets: if the supply cannot be obtained, engineering work that raises per-replica throughput (a new quantisation, speculative decoding, a better router) is valued in GPUs saved and scheduled accordingly.

Figure 9.6 lays the process out as a cycle. Two practices distinguish good processes from poor ones. They measure forecast accuracy—how often actual peaks exceeded each forecast quantile—and correct the forecasts' calibration, because a process that is consistently surprised in one direction wastes money or causes outages. And they make the conversion factors explicit and owned: tokens per second per GPU for each model and configuration is a number that someone measures, publishes and is responsible for, because a 20% error in it is a 20% error in a purchase worth tens of millions of dollars.

Looking ahead

We now know how to decide how much capacity to hold and on which terms, how to forecast the demand that drives it, when preemptible capacity is safe, how to size warm pools and what to do when capacity runs out. Throughout, we took prices as given: $2.50 for a committed GPU-hour, $6.90 on demand, $1.00 spot. Where do those numbers come from, and what would it cost to own the GPUs instead? How should the cost of a fleet be turned into a price per million tokens, what do providers charge and why are input, cached input and output tokens priced differently, and how does an organisation attribute GPU costs to the teams and customers that cause them? Chapter 10 answers these questions of inference economics.

Chapter summary

  • GPU capacity must be planned: lead times are months, demand is correlated across customers, on-demand availability is not guaranteed, and being short is far costlier than being long.
  • Purchase options: on-demand (1.0×, may be unavailable), spot/preemptible (≈ 0.1–0.4×, reclaimable with 30 s–2 min notice), capacity blocks (booked windows), reserved/committed (≈ 0.4–0.6×, 1–3 years), long-term dedicated contracts.
  • Optimal committed capacity (Proposition 9.1): commit to the level demand exceeds a fraction \(c_r/c_o\) of the time. Scarcity of on-demand and the value of lending troughs push the commitment to near the peak.
  • Forecast in GPU-relevant units (prefill and decode tokens per model after cache hits) and in quantiles; provision at the quantile \(c_{\text{short}}/(c_{\text{short}}+c_{\text{over}})\) (the pinball-loss optimum); check calibration.
  • Without checkpoints, preemptible jobs slow down as \((e^{\lambda_pL}-1)/(\lambda_pL)\); with frequent checkpoints the slowdown is about \(1+\lambda_p\tau_c/2\). Batch inference and rollouts suit spot; online serving can use spot as a diversified overlay on a committed base.
  • Warm pools bridge node provisioning: \(w\ge\dot\lambda t_{\text{node}}\) plus failure-domain spare; lending warm capacity to preemptible work recovers its cost; sharing capacity across tiers regains multiplexing.
  • When short: spill to other regions, run hotter, shed deferrable work, degrade model or features, prioritise tenants—each a tested switch.
  • Capacity planning is a quarterly cycle: demand plan → supply requirement (measured throughput) → procurement → operation → measurement → efficiency targets.

Warm-up Compare the main ways of buying cloud GPU capacity.

On-demand: no commitment, list price, availability not guaranteed for scarce GPUs. Spot/preemptible: 60–90% discounts, reclaimable with 30 s–2 min notice, availability fluctuates. Capacity blocks (EC2 Capacity Blocks, GCP Dynamic Workload Scheduler calendar mode): guaranteed instances for days–weeks booked ahead. Reserved/committed-use and savings plans: 1–3 year take-or-pay, often 40–60% off, capacity assured. Long-term dedicated contracts with clouds or neoclouds: multi-year, large, lowest effective price. Typical portfolio: committed base, blocks for events, on-demand and spot for bursts and batch.

Warm-up Why can't a GPU serving fleet just "scale on demand" like a web service?

On-demand GPU capacity is often unavailable for large instances in popular regions, especially at peak hours when everyone needs it; quotas limit what an account can launch; node provisioning takes minutes and replica cold starts more; and on-demand is several times the price of committed capacity. So the base load and most of the peak must be secured in advance (reservations, contracts), with on-demand and spot as supplements and warm pools to bridge provisioning delays.

Core Demand peaks at 2,500 GPUs and troughs at 1,150. Reserved costs $2.50/GPU-h, on-demand $6.90. How many should we reserve?

Cost \(C(R)=c_rTR+c_o\int\max(D-R,0)\) is convex; optimum where demand exceeds \(R\) a fraction \(c_r/c_o=0.36\) of the time. With sinusoidal demand (mean 1,825, amplitude 675), \(R^*\approx1825+675\cos(0.362\pi)\approx2{,}108\) GPUs; daily cost about $142k versus $150k reserving the peak and $302k all on-demand. But adjust: if on-demand may be unavailable at peak (scarcity penalty) or troughs can be lent to batch work, the optimum moves to about the peak (≈ 2,460 in the worked example). Also consider growth and forecast uncertainty over the commitment term, and the flatness of the curve near the optimum.

Core Can we serve production traffic on spot GPUs? How?

Yes, as an overlay, with care. Keep committed capacity that meets the SLO (at a degraded utilisation) if all spot disappears; add spot replicas for normal-load efficiency. Diversify spot across instance types, zones and regions to reduce correlated reclaims. Handle the notice: on interruption signal, fail readiness, stop admitting, finish short requests, resume long streams elsewhere (prefix cache makes resends cheap), and have fast cold starts for replacements. Prefer small (1-GPU) replicas; avoid spot for multi-node replicas whose failure probability multiplies. Quantify: e.g. 71 of 427 GPUs on spot saved about 17% of a tier's cost. Batch inference and rollouts are the natural spot workloads.

It must cover demand growth during node provisioning: \(w\ge\dot\lambda t_{\text{node}}\) (e.g. 15 GPUs/min × 6 min = 90 GPUs), plus replica-level headroom for cold start and spare for the largest failure domain. Deep-dive Extend the newsvendor analysis to include on-demand unavailability and the value of idle capacity.

Marginal analysis: increasing \(R\) by one GPU costs \(c_r\) per hour; when demand exceeds \(R\) (fraction \(f\) of time) it saves the alternative's cost \(c_o+p_{\text{na}}c_{\text{short}}\) (on-demand price plus expected shortfall penalty when on-demand is unavailable); when demand is below \(R\), an idle committed GPU can be lent for value \(c_{\text{idle}}\), offsetting cost. Optimal \(f\) satisfies \(f(c_o+p_{\text{na}}c_{\text{short}}-c_r)=(1-f)(c_r-c_{\text{idle}})\), i.e. \(f^*=(c_r-c_{\text{idle}})/(c_o+p_{\text{na}}c_{\text{short}}-c_{\text{idle}})\). With 2.50, 1.25, 6.90, 0.3, 20: \(f^*\approx0.107\) → commitment ≈ 2,460 of a 2,500 peak. With uncertain future demand curves, take expectations over scenarios, which also favours robustness near the flat optimum.

Deep-dive Your quarterly capacity plan was 25% short at peak. What went wrong, and how do you fix the process?

Possible causes: forecast miss (growth or launch underestimated; forecast in requests rather than tokens; reasoning or long-context features raising tokens per request); conversion error (measured per-replica throughput optimistic—different traffic mix, lower cache hits, engine regression); spare and headroom underestimated (more failures, slower cold starts); procurement shortfall (on-demand unavailable, delayed contracts). Fix: post-mortem decomposing the miss into demand units, conversion factors and supply; forecast in token units with quantiles and calibration tracking; capacity review for product changes; owned, continuously measured throughput factors per model and hardware; plan to a quantile with explicit risk appetite; pre-negotiated burst options (capacity blocks, second provider); graceful-degradation switches tested before peaks.

Deep-dive How do correlated spot reclaims affect how much spot a serving fleet can use?

If reclaims were independent, the number lost at once would be binomial and small relative to the pool, so a little spare would cover it. In practice reclaims correlate: when a region's demand spikes, the provider reclaims many instances of a type at once. Model the loss as a mixture: with probability \(p_b\) a "bad event" removes a fraction \(\phi_b\) of a pool. The committed base must meet the SLO after losing \(\phi_b\) of the spot capacity in the largest correlated pool. Diversification across instance types, zones and regions (weakly correlated pools) reduces the worst-case loss fraction. Measure historical reclaim correlation per pool; cap spot share per pool; treat spot as an efficiency overlay, never as SLO-critical capacity.

Design Design the capacity strategy for a new LLM API launching in three months with uncertain demand.

Scenario forecasts (low/median/high) in tokens per model; minimum footprint per region for availability. Before launch: book capacity blocks covering the launch window at the high scenario (guaranteed, short commitment), plus modest reservations for the low scenario as base; qualify two hardware types and two regions/providers for flexibility; prepare spot and serverless overflow for batch and burst. Engineering: fast cold starts, warm pools, autoscaling on token signals, graceful degradation ladder (smaller model, queueing, prioritise paying tiers), rate limits for free tier. After launch: measure actual demand and per-replica throughput, convert blocks into reservations sized by the critical ratio as uncertainty narrows, and lend troughs to internal batch work.

Design How would you run a capacity-planning process for a fleet of 50,000 GPUs?

Quarterly cycle with monthly updates. Inputs: demand plans by product, model, tier and region in token units with quantiles; known launches and contracts; owned conversion factors (tokens/s per replica per hardware and configuration) measured continuously. Compute supply requirement including spares (failure rates, domain sizes), rollout surge, cold-start headroom. Compare with current and contracted supply; decide procurement by term and lead time (long-term contracts and reservations for base, blocks for events, on-demand/spot for burst), with power and datacenter constraints for owned capacity. Governance: risk appetite (planning quantile), approvals, budgets. Feedback: forecast calibration, utilisation by class, cost per token, efficiency roadmap valued in GPUs. Publish dashboards and decisions.

Core What is the right planning quantile for peak capacity, and how do you derive it?

Treat committed capacity as a newsvendor decision under forecast uncertainty: if a unit of capacity left unused costs \(c_{\text{over}}\) and a unit of shortfall costs \(c_{\text{short}}\) (net of what committing would have cost), plan at quantile \(q=c_{\text{short}}/(c_{\text{short}}+c_{\text{over}})\). E.g. over-cost $2.50, net shortfall $10.50 → \(q\approx0.81\); for a normal forecast \(N(2900,300^2)\) that is about 3,160 GPUs. Higher shortfall costs (SLA penalties, reputational damage) push towards the 95th–99th percentile. Ensure forecasts are calibrated so the quantile means what it says.

Deep-dive Explain the interaction between capacity commitment and cluster sharing.

If committed capacity is idle in troughs, its effective cost per useful hour is high, which argues for committing below the peak and buying peak capacity on demand. But if troughs can be lent to preemptible work worth \(c_{\text{idle}}\) per GPU-hour (batch inference, training, rollouts, evaluations), the effective cost of committing falls and the optimal commitment rises towards the peak. Meanwhile on-demand GPUs are scarce, adding a shortfall risk. Together these mean: commit close to the peak, run a shared cluster where inference has priority and reclaims from borrowers, and invest in reclamation speed (preemption notice, cold starts). Capacity planning, cluster scheduling and cold-start engineering are one optimisation.

Core List the rungs of a graceful-degradation ladder for a capacity shortfall and how you would trigger them.

(1) Spill to other regions or providers with headroom (automatic, subject to latency and residency); (2) raise utilisation targets within SLO slack (automatic, bounded); (3) pause batch, evaluation and rollout work on shared capacity (automatic reclaim); (4) degrade: route free tier or non-critical traffic to smaller models, cap output length, disable expensive modes (policy-driven); (5) prioritise tenants and reject the rest with 429/503 and retry-after (policy-driven). Trigger by SLO-based metrics (TTFT percentiles, queue depth, KV pressure) with hysteresis; each rung has an owner, a runbook, and is exercised in game days.

Warm-up Why does the unit of LLM demand make forecasting harder than for web traffic?

Web capacity scales roughly with request rate. LLM capacity scales with prefill and decode tokens per model after cache hits, which depend on prompt and output lengths, conversation depth, cache-hit rate and model mix—quantities that change with product decisions (reasoning modes, new templates, model tier migrations) independently of request counts. A flat request forecast can hide a doubling of GPU demand. Forecast token units, track the conversion drivers explicitly, and put product changes through capacity review.
