Multi-Region Serving and Disaster Recovery

A service with users on every continent, legal obligations about where their data may go, GPU capacity scattered across whichever regions have it, and a need to survive the loss of an entire region runs in several regions at once. This chapter explains how traffic is steered among regions, how to decide which region serves which users, how much capacity follow-the-sun pooling saves and residency rules cost, which state must follow a user when traffic moves, and how to design, size and test failover for an LLM service.

Why this chapter

Chapter 11 followed a request into one region. A global service has to answer a prior question for every request: which region? The answer balances latency (users want nearby servers), capacity (the nearest region may be full or may lack the right GPUs), cost (power and GPU prices vary by region), law (some data may be processed only in some jurisdictions) and reliability (a region can fail). For classical web services, the latency term usually dominates and regions are chosen for proximity. For LLM services, Chapter 2 showed that tens of milliseconds of network delay are small next to seconds of generation, so capacity, cost and law weigh more—and that changes the architecture.

Multi-region design is also where reliability engineering meets geography. Regions do fail: cloud providers have had region-wide incidents lasting hours, caused by power events, network misconfigurations and failures of internal control-plane services on which everything else depended. A service that runs in one region is only as available as that region; one that runs in several must decide how much capacity to hold in reserve, which state to replicate, and how to move traffic when a region degrades. Interviewers ask exactly this: "design a multi-region LLM API", "what happens to prefix caches when you fail over?", "how much extra capacity do you need to survive losing a region?", "a customer's data must stay in the EU—what changes?".

We start with why services go multi-region (§12.1) and how traffic is steered (§12.2). We formulate region selection as an optimisation and quantify follow-the-sun pooling (§12.3), then add data-residency constraints (§12.4). We examine the state that must follow users (§12.5), failover architectures and their recovery objectives (§12.6), capacity for regional failure (§12.7), and how to test it all (§12.8).

12.1 Why more than one region

Five forces push an LLM service into several regions, and they pull in different directions. Latency: a user in Singapore talking to a region in Virginia pays about 200 ms of round trip per exchange; for chat this is tolerable, for voice and many-step agents it is not (§2.7). Capacity: GPUs, especially the newest, are available in some regions and not others, quotas are per region, and no single region may offer enough capacity for a large fleet (Chapter 9). Cost: GPU prices, power prices and the carbon intensity of electricity differ by region, so moving latency-tolerant work to cheaper regions saves money. Residency: laws and contracts may require that some customers' prompts, outputs and stored data be processed and kept in particular jurisdictions (§12.4). Reliability: a region is a failure domain (Chapter 1), and surviving its loss requires capacity elsewhere.

The forces conflict. Latency argues for many regions near users; capacity and cost argue for few, large regions where power and GPUs are plentiful; residency argues for regions in particular countries regardless of their capacity; reliability argues for at least two or three regions, each able to absorb another's load. A typical large LLM service ends up with a small number of large GPU regions carrying most of the work, a larger number of smaller regions for residency-bound and latency-sensitive traffic, and a global edge that terminates connections near users and carries requests over the provider's backbone. 12.2 Steering traffic between regions

Three mechanisms steer users to regions, often in combination. DNS-based global load balancing answers the query for the API's host name with an address in the region chosen for the querying client, using the location of the client's DNS resolver, measured latencies, region health and configured weights. It is simple and universal, but it is coarse and slow: the decision is made per resolver rather than per user, and answers are cached by resolvers and clients for their time-to-live, so a change takes at least a TTL—often much longer, because some resolvers ignore short TTLs—to take effect. DNS is therefore good for steady-state placement and poor for fast failover.

Anycast announces the same IP address from many locations through the internet's routing protocol, BGP, so that each user's packets go to the "nearest" location in routing terms. Failover is faster—withdrawing an announcement redirects traffic within seconds to minutes as routes converge—and it absorbs denial-of-service attacks by spreading them. But BGP chooses routes by policy and path length, not by latency or load, so anycast alone cannot balance load or respect residency.

The common modern design combines anycast with edge proxies: anycast brings each user's connection to a nearby point of presence, where a proxy terminates TLS (§11.1) and forwards the request over the provider's private backbone to a region chosen per request by a global traffic manager using live data on each region's health, capacity and queue depths, the request's latency sensitivity, and the tenant's residency policy. Per-request steering at the edge reacts in seconds, can split a single user's traffic between regions, and can move load away from a region that is degraded but not dead—the most common situation in practice.

For the mathematician — why "nearest" in BGP is not nearest in latency

The internet is a graph of autonomous systems (networks run by one organisation) joined by peering and transit links. BGP lets each system announce which address prefixes it can reach and through which sequence of systems; each router chooses among announced routes by a lexicographic order of policies—business relationships first, then shortest AS-path length, then tie-breakers—not by measured delay. Two paths with the same AS-path length can differ by tens of milliseconds, and a policy preference can send traffic across an ocean when a nearby path exists. Anycast therefore gives a map from users to sites that is roughly, but not reliably, latency-minimising, and that can change when any network on the path changes its policy. Measurement-driven steering at the application layer corrects for this by observing actual latencies and choosing destinations explicitly.

12.3 Which region should serve which users?

Suppose demand originates in client areas \(i\) (cities, countries or edge sites) at rates \(\lambda_i\), and can be served in regions \(j\) with capacity \(C_j\) (in requests per second at the target utilisation, or better, in GPU-equivalents of prefill and decode work), cost \(c_j\) per unit of work, and round-trip latency \(\ell_{ij}\) between area and region. Let \(x_{ij}\) be the rate of demand from \(i\) served in \(j\), and let \(\mathcal A_i\) be the set of regions allowed for area \(i\)'s traffic (by residency or contract). The placement that minimises cost plus a monetised latency penalty \(\omega_\ell\) per millisecond solves the linear programme

\[ \min_{x\ge0}\ \sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\bigl(c_j+\omega_\ell\,\ell_{ij}\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_jx_{ij}=\lambda_i,\quad\sum_ix_{ij}\le C_j,\quad x_{ij}=0\ \text{for}\ j\notin\mathcal A_i . \]

The programme is a transportation problem, a classical special case of linear programming whose solutions are integral and efficiently computable, and its dual variables are informative: the shadow price of region \(j\)'s capacity constraint says how much the objective would fall with one more unit of capacity there, which is exactly what a capacity planner wants to know (Chapter 9). In practice a traffic manager solves a version of it every few seconds with current demand, capacity and latencies, and writes the solution as routing weights for the edges.

The programme's most important feature for LLMs is the size of \(\omega_\ell\). For a web page, a 100 ms penalty is significant; for an LLM response that takes 10 s, a 100 ms change in the first byte is a 1% change in total time, and users barely notice. So for most text traffic the latency term is small relative to cost and capacity, and the optimum sends traffic across continents when a distant region has spare capacity or cheaper GPUs. The exception is latency-critical traffic—voice, real-time agents, code completion—for which \(\omega_\ell\) is large, and that traffic stays near users. A traffic manager therefore distinguishes classes of traffic with different latency weights.

The payoff of letting text traffic travel is follow-the-sun pooling. Each continent's demand follows its own working day, so the regions' peaks fall at different times. If each region must provision for its own peak, total capacity is the sum of the regional peaks; if demand can be served anywhere, total capacity need only cover the peak of the global sum, which is much flatter. Figure 12.2 shows this for Atlas, with demand split 45% Americas, 35% Europe and 20% Asia-Pacific, each following a daily cycle with a peak 60% above its mean, at peaks 8 hours apart.

Figure 12.2 quantifies an advantage that only global services enjoy. If each region served only its own users, Atlas would need capacity equal to 1.6 times its global mean demand (the sum of the regional peaks); with full pooling it needs 1.13 times, a saving of 29%—comparable to everything a good engine optimisation can deliver. The saving can be derived in closed form: if region \(k\) has weight \(w_k\) and demand \(w_k(1+a_{\text{d}}\cos(2\pi(\tau-\phi_k)/24))\), the global total is \(1+a_{\text{d}}\,|\sum_kw_ke^{2\pi i\phi_k/24}|\cos(\cdot)\), so its peak-to-mean ratio is \(1+a_{\text{d}}|\sum_kw_ke^{2\pi i\phi_k/24}|\); with three equal regions eight hours apart the sum vanishes and global demand is flat. Real demand is lumpier, and weekends and holidays differ by country, but the effect is large. Two caveats limit it: latency-critical traffic cannot travel, and residency rules forbid some traffic from travelling at all.

Worked example — routing Atlas's evening peak

Situation. At 19:00 UTC, the Americas are at their peak and need 1,450 GPU-equivalents of 70B capacity; US regions hold 1,250 at target utilisation. Europe is past its peak, using 650 of its 900; Asia-Pacific uses 180 of 400.

Without pooling. The US regions are short by 200 GPU-equivalents: either they run hot (latency rises sharply, §11.6), or they buy on-demand capacity at several times the price (if available, Chapter 9), or they shed load.

With pooling. The traffic manager sends 200 units of latency-tolerant US chat traffic to Europe (about 80 ms extra round trip, under 1% of a 10 s response) and keeps voice, agents and code completion in the US. Europe rises to 850 of 900. No traffic from EU-resident tenants moves; no US traffic covered by US-only residency contracts moves.

Cost. Cross-region transfer of request and response bytes is negligible (§4.8). The lost prefix-cache hits for moved conversations raise prefill for that traffic (§12.5), which the European capacity must absorb—so the traffic manager prefers to move new conversations, whose caches do not yet exist, rather than continuing ones.

Drill 12.2 — A small transportation problem

Two client areas, Americas (demand 1,000 units) and Europe (600), can be served in US (capacity 900, cost 1.0 per unit) or EU (capacity 900, cost 1.2). Round trips: Americas–US 30 ms, Americas–EU 110 ms, Europe–EU 20 ms, Europe–US 100 ms. Latency costs \(\omega_\ell=0.001\) per unit per ms for chat. What is the optimal assignment? What changes if 300 units of European demand are EU-resident?

Solution. Per-unit total costs: Am→US \(1.0+0.03=1.03\); Am→EU \(1.2+0.11=1.31\); Eu→EU \(1.2+0.02=1.22\); Eu→US \(1.0+0.10=1.10\). Total demand 1,600 against 1,800 capacity. The US is cheaper for both areas, so fill it first with the area that gains most from it: Americas saves 0.28 per unit by using the US instead of the EU, Europe saves 0.12, so the US takes 900 units of Americas demand; the remaining 100 Americas units and all 600 European units go to the EU (700 of 900). Cost: \(900\times1.03+100\times1.31+600\times1.22=927+131+732=1{,}790\). With 300 European units resident, nothing changes, since all European demand is already served in the EU. If instead the US had spare capacity (say 1,300), the optimum would move 300 non-resident European units to the US (saving 0.12 each) and keep the resident 300 in the EU—the residency constraint then costs \(300\times0.12=36\).

12.4 Data residency and sovereignty

Many customers—governments, banks, healthcare providers, and companies subject to data-protection law—require that their data be processed and stored within particular jurisdictions. The legal landscape is complex and changes, and engineers should rely on their organisation's lawyers rather than on any textbook, but its broad shape matters for architecture. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation restricts transfers of personal data outside the European Economic Area unless specific safeguards apply; sector rules such as health-data and financial-services regulations impose their own controls; several countries require certain categories of data to remain within their borders; and governments increasingly ask for sovereign clouds whose operations, staff and encryption keys are also under local control. Cloud providers responded with region-scoped services, data-boundary commitments and sovereign offerings, and LLM API providers with regional endpoints and data-residency options.

For an LLM service, residency is broader than "which region runs the model". A request's data appears in many places: the prompt and response in transit and in the engine's memory; the KV cache and prefix cache, which are a derived representation of the prompt; logs and traces, which may contain prompts or excerpts; conversation state and uploaded files stored for multi-turn use; vector stores built from customer documents (Chapter 16); fine-tuned models and adapters trained on customer data; evaluation and abuse-monitoring pipelines that sample traffic; and backups. A residency commitment must cover all of them. Figure 12.3 shows the boundary for an EU-resident tenant.

Residency has two engineering costs. The first is capacity: a residency-bound tenant's traffic cannot join follow-the-sun pooling, so its region must be provisioned for its own peak, plus a failover reserve that must also lie inside the boundary. If the EU tier's peak is 1.6 times its mean and it must survive the loss of one of its two regions, it needs \(1.6\times2=3.2\) times its mean demand in installed capacity, against about 1.13 times for pooled global traffic with spare capacity shared across many regions—nearly three times as much per unit of demand. Providers recover some of this by sharing residency-bound capacity with non-resident traffic that happens to originate inside the boundary and by lending its troughs to resident batch work. The second cost is complexity: every component in Figure 12.3 must know a tenant's residency and enforce it, the global traffic manager must never route resident traffic out, and logging, monitoring and support processes must avoid copying content across the boundary. Residency is therefore best designed in as a tenant attribute that flows with every request, not retrofitted.

12.5 State that must follow users

A stateless service can move requests between regions freely. An LLM service is less stateless than it looks, and the state it holds determines what happens when traffic moves. Table 12.1 lists the main kinds.

Figure 12.4 shows the arrangement that most multi-region LLM services adopt for this state. Each user or conversation has a home region, recorded in a small global directory or encoded in the conversation's identifier, and requests are routed home whenever the home region is healthy, so that its caches, conversation state and files are local. Durable state is replicated asynchronously to a partner region inside the same residency boundary. When the home region fails, the traffic manager sends the conversation to the partner, which has a copy of its durable state—missing at most the last few seconds—but none of its soft state, so the first turn after failover pays a full prefill. When the home recovers, conversations either return or are re-homed, depending on how much traffic has accumulated at the partner.

The most interesting entry is the first, because it is unique to LLMs and its cost is easy to underestimate. A prefix cache is soft state: losing it never makes an answer wrong, only slower and more expensive. But Atlas's traffic depends on it heavily: 60% of input tokens are cache hits (Chapter 1). When a region fails and its continuing conversations move elsewhere, every one of them arrives at a replica that has never seen its history, and its whole prompt must be prefilled. The receiving region therefore faces not only the moved demand but an inflated version of it.

Worked example — the cache cost of failing over

Setup. US-East fails at 15:00 UTC while serving 1,500 requests/s of Atlas's 70B tier; US-West absorbs them. Normally each request has 2,000 input tokens with 60% cached, so 800 uncached; after failover, continuing conversations find no cache in US-West.

Prefill demand of the moved traffic. In the first minutes, essentially all 2,000 tokens per request must be prefilled: \(1500\times2000=3\times10^{6}\) tokens/s instead of \(1500\times800=1.2\times10^{6}\), two and a half times as much. At 25,000 prefill tokens/s per replica, that is 120 replica-equivalents of prefill instead of 48.

Recovery. As conversations take their next turns, US-West's caches fill; with a typical turn interval of a minute or two, the hit rate recovers over several minutes, and the excess prefill decays roughly exponentially with that time constant.

Design consequences. Failover capacity must include a prefill surge allowance (here about 70 extra replica-equivalents for a few minutes), or the receiving region's TTFT will spike exactly when it is most stressed; disaggregated prefill pools that can borrow decode capacity help; replicating hot prefix caches across regions (for example, shared system prompts) removes part of the surge, at the cost of cross-region bandwidth.

Durable state needs replication with explicit guarantees, and two numbers describe them. The recovery point objective (RPO) is how much recent data may be lost in a disaster—the replication lag of asynchronous copies. The recovery time objective (RTO) is how long the service may be unavailable before it recovers. Conversation threads and uploaded files are typically replicated asynchronously between regions, giving an RPO of seconds to minutes: a region failure may lose the last turn of a conversation, which the client can resend. Synchronous cross-region replication would give an RPO of zero at the price of adding a cross-region round trip to every write, which is rarely worth it for conversation state. Configuration, model registries and quotas are small and change slowly, and can be replicated strongly. What must not happen is that durable state for a region lives only in that region, because then the region's failure becomes data loss.

12.6 Failover architectures and recovery objectives

Disaster-recovery designs are conventionally arranged by how much capacity is kept running in the standby location, and therefore by their RTO and cost. Figure 12.5 compares the three main patterns.

For an interactive LLM service, Figure 12.5's middle and right patterns carry a risk that ordinary services do not face: capacity. Scaling up a standby region requires GPUs, and during a large regional outage every affected customer of the cloud is trying to obtain GPUs in the neighbouring regions at the same moment. A standby plan that depends on on-demand GPUs is a plan that may fail exactly when it is needed (Chapter 9). Active-active designs avoid this by holding the failover capacity running—serving traffic in normal operation—which is static stability (§1.7) applied at the regional level. The cost of that spare capacity is the subject of the next section; it can be reduced by lending it to preemptible work (Chapter 7) that is evicted when failover traffic arrives.

Within a region, the same logic of failure domains applies at a finer grain, and many large services adopt a cell-based architecture. A region is divided into several independent cells, each a complete copy of the serving stack—gateways, routers, replicas, caches, state stores—serving a fixed subset of tenants, assigned by a thin routing layer. A bug, a bad configuration push, a poisonous request or a noisy tenant can then damage only its cell, not the region; changes are rolled out cell by cell (Chapter 13); and each cell's size can be chosen so that its loss is absorbed by the others. Cells cost some efficiency, since capacity is pooled within a cell rather than across the region, but for services whose availability matters more than the last few percent of utilisation, the bounded blast radius is worth it. For LLM serving, cells also bound the reach of shared caches, which is useful for isolation (Chapter 14).

Failover also needs a decision, and the decision has its own failure modes. Automatic failover reacts quickly but can be fooled: a monitoring outage, a network partition between the monitor and a healthy region, or a brief degradation can trigger an unnecessary evacuation, which itself causes load spikes and cache losses elsewhere. Manual failover is slower but deliberate. A common compromise is automatic load shifting—the traffic manager moves load away from a region whose health and latency metrics degrade, proportionally and reversibly—combined with a human decision for a full evacuation. Equally important is that failover must not depend on the failed region: a traffic manager, DNS zone, identity service or configuration store anchored in one region turns that region's failure into a global one. Several widely reported cloud outages, including a long us-east-1 incident in October 2025 that began with DNS resolution failures for an internal database service, affected services far outside the failing region because global functions depended on it.

12.7 Capacity to survive a regional failure

How much spare capacity does surviving the loss of a region require? The calculation mirrors the zone-spreading argument of §7.4. If a service's demand is spread evenly across \(n_{\text{reg}}\) regions and must still be served at the target utilisation after any one region is lost, each surviving region must be able to carry \(1/(n_{\text{reg}}-1)\) of the demand, so total installed capacity must be \(n_{\text{reg}}/(n_{\text{reg}}-1)\) times what the demand needs. If, during a failover, the service may run hotter—at 90% utilisation instead of its normal 75%, accepting worse tail latency for the duration—the factor shrinks by \(0.75/0.9\). Figure 12.6 plots both.

Figure 12.6 explains why large services run in several regions rather than two: the spare capacity for regional failure falls from 100% to tens of percent. Three refinements matter in practice. Regions are not equal: if one region carries 45% of the traffic, as US-East does for Atlas, the others must absorb 45%, so the spare required is set by the largest region, which argues for keeping regions similar in size. Residency boundaries partition the problem: the EU's two regions must cover each other without help from outside, so they need the expensive two-region factor regardless of how many regions exist globally. And the prefix-cache surge of §12.5 adds a transient on top: for some minutes after failover, the receiving regions need more prefill capacity than the steady-state calculation suggests. A realistic plan combines spread across several similar regions, a tolerance for running hot during failovers, lending of spare capacity to preemptible work, and graceful degradation (Chapter 9) for the cases the spare cannot cover.

Drill 12.1 — Regional spare for Atlas

Atlas's 70B tier needs 2,500 GPUs of capacity at peak at 75% utilisation (Chapter 1), spread as US-East 45%, US-West 25%, EU 20% (two regions, residency-bound) and Asia-Pacific 10%. Non-EU traffic may fail over among the non-EU regions; EU traffic only within the EU. During failovers, utilisation may rise to 90%. How much installed capacity does each group need to survive the loss of any one region?

Solution. Non-EU demand: 80% of 2,500 = 2,000 GPUs at 75%. Losing US-East (the largest, 1,125) leaves US-West and Asia-Pacific, which must carry 2,000 GPU-equivalents of demand at 90%, i.e. \(2000\times0.75/0.9\approx1{,}667\) GPUs installed between them, plus their own regions must already hold what they normally serve. If installed capacity is proportional to normal share, total non-EU installed \(X\) must satisfy \(X\times(0.35/0.80)\ge1{,}667\), so \(X\ge3{,}810\): about 1.9 times the demand, because one region is so large. Rebalancing to US-East 35%, US-West 30%, Asia 15% lowers the requirement to \(X\times(0.45/0.8)\ge1{,}667\), \(X\approx2{,}960\), about 1.48 times. EU: 500 GPUs of demand across two regions; each must carry all of it at 90%: \(500\times0.75/0.9\approx417\) GPUs per region, 833 installed, 1.67 times. Lesson: region sizes and residency boundaries dominate the cost of regional redundancy.

12.8 Testing disaster recovery

A failover design that has never been exercised is a hypothesis. Experience across the industry is consistent: the first real regional failover of an untested design uncovers dependencies nobody knew about—a configuration service anchored in the failed region, a cache that cannot warm fast enough, quotas in the receiving region that block scale-up, a runbook that refers to a dashboard hosted in the failed region. Mature organisations therefore test disaster recovery deliberately and regularly. Game days rehearse a failure scenario with the people who would respond, in a staging environment or in production at low risk. Region evacuation drills move all traffic out of a production region for an hour, under controlled conditions, and measure whether the others absorb it within SLO. Chaos engineering, popularised by Netflix's Chaos Monkey (2011), injects failures continuously—killing instances, adding latency, partitioning networks—so that resilience is tested every day rather than discovered during an incident.

For LLM services, drills should measure the quantities this chapter introduced: how quickly the traffic manager shifts load, how far TTFT rises in the receiving regions during the prefix-cache surge, whether reserved GPU capacity and quotas suffice, whether residency constraints are respected during the move (EU traffic stays in the EU), how long durable state takes to become available, and whether the control plane remains fully functional. The results feed back into capacity planning (Chapter 9) and into the reliability practice of the next chapter, which treats failures of all sizes, from one GPU to one region.

Looking ahead

We now know how to steer traffic among regions, how to place demand with latency, cost, capacity and residency in view, how much follow-the-sun pooling saves, what state must move with users and how failover is designed and sized. A regional outage, though, is a rare and dramatic event. Most failures are small and constant: a GPU's memory develops errors, a node falls off the network, a software rollout introduces a regression, a dependency slows down. At the scale of thousands of GPUs these happen every day. How should reliability be defined and measured for an LLM service, how often do GPUs actually fail, how are failures detected and contained, and how are changes rolled out safely? Chapter 13 develops reliability engineering for AI services.

Chapter summary

  • Multi-region serving balances latency, capacity, cost, residency and reliability; LLM text traffic tolerates tens of milliseconds, so capacity, cost and law weigh more than for web traffic.
  • Traffic steering: DNS global load balancing (coarse, slowed by caching), anycast (fast but BGP picks routes by policy, not latency), and anycast plus edge proxies with a global traffic manager choosing regions per request.
  • Region assignment is a transportation linear programme minimising cost plus a latency penalty subject to capacity and residency; dual prices value capacity per region. Latency weights differ by traffic class.
  • Follow-the-sun pooling: global peak-to-mean \(=1+a_{\text{d}}|\sum_kw_ke^{2\pi i\phi_k/24}|\); for Atlas, 1.13 versus 1.6 for per-region peaks, about 29% less capacity.
  • Residency covers prompts, KV and prefix caches, logs, conversations, files, vector stores, fine-tunes, monitoring samples and backups; resident traffic cannot pool and must fail over within its boundary, raising capacity needs.
  • LLM services hold state: prefix caches (soft, but losing them on failover multiplies prefill, ≈ 2.5× for Atlas), conversations and files (replicate with an RPO), adapters, quotas, batch progress.
  • Active-active (running spare, RTO seconds–minutes), active-passive (scale up standby, GPU-availability risk), pilot light (rebuild, hours). Failover must not depend on the failed region; prefer automatic load shifting plus deliberate evacuation.
  • Spare to survive one region's loss: \(n_{\text{reg}}/(n_{\text{reg}}-1)\), reduced by tolerating higher utilisation during failover; largest-region share and residency boundaries dominate; add the cache surge.
  • Test DR with game days, evacuation drills and chaos engineering, measuring shift speed, TTFT surge, capacity, residency compliance and control-plane independence.

Warm-up Compare DNS-based global load balancing with anycast.

DNS GSLB returns region-specific addresses per resolver based on location, latency, health and weights; universal and simple, but coarse (per resolver, not user) and slow to change (TTL caching, resolvers that ignore TTLs). Anycast announces the same IP from many sites via BGP; users reach a routing-nearest site; failover in seconds to minutes as routes converge; absorbs DDoS; but BGP picks routes by policy and AS-path length, not latency or load, and cannot express residency. Modern designs use anycast to edge proxies plus a global traffic manager that chooses the region per request.

Warm-up Define RTO and RPO and give values appropriate to an LLM chat service.

RTO (recovery time objective): maximum acceptable time the service is unavailable after a disaster. RPO (recovery point objective): maximum acceptable data loss, as time of recent writes lost. For an LLM chat API, active-active serving gives RTO of seconds to a few minutes (time to shift traffic and absorb the cache surge); conversation threads and files replicated asynchronously give RPO of seconds to a minute (the last turn may need resending); configuration and registries replicated strongly (near-zero RPO). Billing and usage records may need stronger guarantees.

Warm-up What does a data-residency requirement change in an LLM service?

All content-derived artefacts must stay in the boundary: request processing, KV and prefix caches, logs and traces with content, conversation state and files, vector stores, fine-tunes and adapters, monitoring and evaluation samples, backups, failover targets, and ideally encryption keys. Routing must never send resident traffic out (traffic manager policy), failover only within the boundary, support access controlled. Capacity rises because resident traffic can't pool globally and must cover its own peak plus in-boundary failover. Implement residency as a tenant attribute enforced everywhere; involve legal review.

Core How much capacity does follow-the-sun pooling save? Derive it.

Model region \(k\) demand as \(w_k(1+a_{\text{d}}\cos(2\pi(\tau-\phi_k)/24))\). Without pooling, capacity is the sum of regional peaks: \((1+a_{\text{d}})\sum w_k=1+a_{\text{d}}\). With pooling, capacity is the global peak: the sum is \(1+a_{\text{d}}\,\mathrm{Re}[e^{2\pi i\tau/24}\sum_kw_ke^{-2\pi i\phi_k/24}]\), peaking at \(1+a_{\text{d}}|\sum_kw_ke^{2\pi i\phi_k/24}|\). For Atlas (45/35/20, peaks 8 h apart, a = 0.6): magnitude ≈ 0.22, global peak ≈ 1.13 vs 1.6, saving ≈ 29%. Equal thirds 8 h apart: flat. Limits: only latency-tolerant, non-resident traffic pools; weekends/holidays differ; cache losses when moving continuing sessions.

Core What happens to prefix caches when you fail a region over, and how do you plan for it?

Caches are local soft state; continuing conversations moved to another region arrive with no cached history, so their whole prompts must be prefilled—with 60% hit rates, prefill demand for moved traffic rises ~2.5× until caches rebuild over the next turns (minutes). The receiving region needs a prefill surge allowance on top of steady-state failover capacity, or TTFT spikes. Mitigate: disaggregated prefill pools that can borrow capacity, prefer moving new sessions during load shifting, replicate hot shared prefixes (system prompts) across regions, admission control and graceful degradation during the surge, and measure the surge in drills.

Core How much spare capacity do we need to survive a regional failure?

For \(n\) equal regions keeping target utilisation, installed capacity = \(n/(n-1)\) × demand: 2× for two, 1.5× for three, 1.25× for five. Allowing higher utilisation during failover (75% → 90%) multiplies by 0.83. Uneven regions: spare is set by the largest region's share. Residency boundaries partition: an EU pair needs the two-region factor regardless of global footprint. Add the transient prefix-cache surge. Reduce cost by spreading over more similar regions, lending spare to preemptible work, and graceful degradation for residual shortfalls.

Core Formulate region selection as an optimisation problem.

Variables \(x_{ij}\) = demand from area \(i\) served in region \(j\). Minimise \(\sum x_{ij}(c_j+\omega_\ell\ell_{ij})\) (cost plus monetised latency) subject to \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\lambda_i\) (serve all demand), \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le C_j\) (capacity at target utilisation), \(x_{ij}=0\) outside allowed sets (residency, contracts). A transportation LP, solved every few seconds by the traffic manager with live demand, capacity and latency; outputs routing weights. Separate classes with different \(\omega_\ell\) (voice high, chat low). Dual prices of capacity constraints feed capacity planning. Add cache-affinity terms (cost of moving continuing sessions) and hysteresis to avoid flapping.

Core Active-active or active-passive for an LLM API? Justify.

Active-active for interactive serving: failover capacity is running and warm (weights loaded, caches partially warm, quotas proven), RTO seconds–minutes, no dependency on obtaining GPUs during a disaster when everyone else is also scrambling; spare can be lent to preemptible work. Active-passive saves standby cost but risks being unable to scale the standby (GPU scarcity, quotas, cold starts of many minutes), so RTO and success are uncertain. Pilot light is acceptable only for low-priority services or batch. Within residency boundaries with only two regions, active-active is still preferred but costs the full 2× factor.

Deep-dive Design the global traffic manager for Atlas.

Inputs every few seconds: per-region health (error rates, TTFT/TPOT percentiles), capacity (spare GPU-equivalents per tier, queue depths), demand by area and class, measured edge-to-region latencies, tenant residency and contracts. Solve per-tier, per-class transportation LPs (or greedy heuristics) with latency weights by class, cache-affinity cost for continuing sessions, and residency constraints; apply hysteresis and rate limits on weight changes to avoid oscillation. Output weights to edge proxies; sessions sticky to their home region unless moved. Degradation: proportional load shedding away from unhealthy regions; full evacuation requires human confirmation. Replicated across regions with static stability (edges keep last weights if the manager is unavailable). Observability and drills.

Deep-dive Which LLM-service state needs replication, with what guarantees?

Model registry and configuration: small, critical—strongly consistent replication across regions. Model weights and adapters: replicate artefacts to all serving regions within each boundary ahead of need (Chapter 5). Conversation threads, uploaded files: asynchronous replication with seconds-level RPO, residency-scoped; route sessions to a home region. Vector stores: replicated indexes or rebuildable from replicated documents (Chapter 16). Quotas and billing: per-region counters with a global ledger; billing records durable (stronger guarantees). Prefix/KV caches: soft—don't replicate generally, but optionally replicate hot shared prefixes. Batch outputs: checkpointed to replicated storage. Logs: residency-scoped, possibly content-redacted for global aggregation.

Deep-dive Why do regional outages often spread globally, and how do you prevent it?

Hidden dependencies on a single region: global DNS zones, identity/auth services, configuration stores, control-plane APIs, container registries, monitoring and dashboards, or internal services whose primary lives in one region; when it fails, every region that calls it fails or cannot change configuration (e.g. cannot scale up to absorb traffic). Prevent: inventory dependencies per region (including transitively), replicate control-plane services across regions with static stability (data planes keep working with last-known state), avoid control-plane calls on the request path, run regional evacuation drills that also cut off the region's control-plane services, and design failover tooling that runs outside the failing region.

Design Design a multi-region LLM API serving US, EU and Asia users with EU residency for some tenants.

Regions: two US, two EU, one or two APAC, similar sizes where possible. Edge: anycast PoPs with TLS, edge proxies, global traffic manager with residency-aware LP routing; EU-resident tenants pinned to EU regions; latency classes. Each region: gateway, routers, model pools for all tiers (some heavy tiers only in large regions), local caches. State: conversations/files replicated asynchronously within boundary; adapters replicated to all serving regions in boundary; registry/config globally replicated. Capacity: active-active with n/(n−1) spare, EU pair with 2× within-boundary spare (lent to EU batch), surge allowance for cache losses. Failover: automatic load shifting, human-confirmed evacuations, no single-region control-plane dependencies. Residency enforcement in logs, monitoring, support. Drills quarterly.

Design A region has spare GPUs but higher latency for most users. When do you route traffic there?

When the monetised latency penalty is smaller than the cost saving or capacity relief: for latency-tolerant chat and batch, an extra 50–150 ms RTT is under 1–2% of response time—route freely, especially at local peaks (follow-the-sun) or when local capacity is short; prefer new sessions to avoid cache losses. For voice, real-time agents, code completion and many-round-trip workflows, keep local. Respect residency. Account for egress (small) and cross-region dependencies (vector stores, files—may need local replicas or must stay home). Encode in per-class latency weights in the traffic manager's optimisation.

Core How would you run a region-evacuation drill for an LLM service?

Pick a low-risk window; announce; define success criteria (SLO maintained in receiving regions, residency respected, shift time, surge size). Gradually shift traffic out via the traffic manager, then full evacuation; also simulate loss of the region's control-plane services. Measure: time to shift, TTFT/TPOT percentiles and errors in receivers, prefill surge from cache loss, autoscaler behaviour and quota limits, GPU capacity headroom, state availability (conversations, files, adapters), dashboards and runbooks reachable. Restore gradually. Postmortem with action items into capacity plans and dependency fixes. Repeat regularly, rotating regions.

Deep-dive How do residency constraints change the economics of multi-region capacity?

Resident traffic cannot join global pooling (lose the ~29% follow-the-sun saving), must be provisioned for its own peak, and must fail over within its boundary, often only two regions (factor 2 instead of n/(n−1) with many regions). Together this can mean ~3× installed capacity per unit of demand versus ~1.1–1.3× for pooled traffic. Mitigations: share resident regions' capacity with non-resident traffic originating locally, lend troughs to resident batch work, offer residency as a priced tier reflecting its cost, add a third in-boundary region if demand justifies it (factor 1.5), and use graceful degradation within the boundary for rare failures.

Warm-up Why do large services run in several regions rather than two?

Spare capacity to survive one region's loss is n/(n−1): 100% extra with two regions, 50% with three, 25% with five. More regions also improve latency for more users, reduce correlated risk, and give access to more GPU capacity and quotas. Costs: more operational complexity, replication, and capacity fragmentation (each region needs minimum footprints per model). Most large services settle on several similar-sized regions plus residency-specific ones.
